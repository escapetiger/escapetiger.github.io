Korn’s inequality asserts the control of the $L^2$ norm of the gradient of a vector field by the $L^2$ norm of its symmetric part, under certain conditions.

Introduction#

Let $\Omega$ be a smooth bounded open set in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $d \ge 2$ to avoid trivial situations. For a vector field $u: \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$, we denote by $\nabla u$ the differential of $u$ with values in $\mathfrak{M}$ where $\mathfrak{M}$ is the set of $d \times d$ real matrices. We also denote by $\nabla^su$ (resp. $\nabla^a u$) the symmetric (resp. antisymmetric) part of $\nabla u$ that takes values in $\mathfrak{M}^s$ (resp. $\mathfrak{M}^a$) where $\mathfrak{M}^s$ (resp. $\mathfrak{M}^a$) is the set of $d \times d$ real symmetric (resp. antisymmetric) matrices. Written with Cartesian coordinates, this mean that

$$ (\nabla u)_{ij} = \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}, \quad (\nabla^su)_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right), \quad (\nabla^a u)_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} - \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right). $$

Moreover, when $u = (u_j)$ and $M = (m_{ij})$ are respectively a vector field and a matrix field on $\Omega$, we use the following notations,

$$ \|u\|_{L^p(\Omega)} = \left(\int_{\Omega}|u|^p\right)^{1/p},\quad \|M\|_{L^p(\Omega)} = \left(\int_{\Omega}|M|^p\right)^{1/p}, $$

where

$$ |u| = \left(\sum_{j} u_j^2\right)^{1/2},\quad |M| = \left(\sum_{ij}m_{ij}^2\right)^{1/2}. $$

The core of this note is the inequality of the following form:

$$ \|\nabla u\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le C(\Omega)\|\nabla^su\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2, \tag{H0} $$

where $C(\Omega) > 0$ is a constant that depends only on the domain $\Omega$.

Application in the mechanics of elastic solid#

The theory of elasticity seeks to answer a fundamental question:

How does a solid body resist deformation and return to its original configuration once the external forces are removed?

In the regime of small deformations, this behavor is described by the Hooke’s law, which asserts that the restoring force depends linearly on the displacement. This linear relation admits a local formulation in terms of stress (internal forces per unit area) and strain (local deformation). The elastic energy arises from the work done by internal forces during deformation; for a linear elastic body, this work is path-independent and leads to an elastic energy given by a quadratic form of the strain.

We now make the above description more precise from a mathematical point of view. The deformation of a body is described by a displacement field $u = u(x)$ which assigns to each reference point $x$ its new position. The gradient of the displacement $\nabla u$ encodes the local behavior of deformation; however, it also includes contributions from rigid-body rotations.

More precisely, since any square matrix can be decomposed into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts, we write

$$ \nabla u = \nabla^su + \nabla^a u. $$

The symmetric part $\nabla^su$, called the strain tensor, accounts for the deformation (stretching, compression, and shear) which modify the size and shape of an infinitesimal element. It is this component that gives rise to the stress that describes forces present during deformation and leads to the storage of elastic energy. In contrast, the antisymmetric part $\nabla^au$, called the spin tensor, represents the rate of rotation, which does not change the shape of the body and hence does not contribute to the elastic energy.

In general, the elastic energy associated with a displacement field $u$ is given by

$$ \mathcal{E}(u) = \int_{\Omega} \mathcal{W}(u)\mathrm{d}x, $$

where $\mathcal{W} : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is the elastic energy density. In the linear regime, the energy density can be expressed as

$$ \mathcal{W}(u) = \frac{1}{2} \sigma(u) : \nabla^s u, $$

In a homogeneous and isotropic body, the stress tensor is given by

$$ \sigma(u) = 2\mu \nabla^su + \lambda(\operatorname{tr}\nabla^s u) I. \tag{1} $$

where $\mu > 0$ measures resistance to shear, and $\lambda > 0$ measures resistance to expansion or compression. A simple computation yields

$$ \mathcal{E}(u) = \mu \int_{\Omega} |\nabla^s u|^2\mathrm{d}x + \frac{\lambda}{2} \int_{\Omega}|\operatorname{tr}\nabla^su|^2\mathrm{d}x. \tag{2} $$

This means that the elastic energy $\mathcal{E}(u)$ provides the control only on the symmetric part $\nabla^su$ of the gradient, namely,

$$ C\|\nabla^su\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le \mathcal{E}(u). \tag{3} $$

However, the regularity and stability of displacement field $u$ are measured in terms of the full gradient $\nabla u$, for instance, through the $H^1$ norm. To address this issue, we need an estimate that allows to recover control of $\nabla u$ from $\nabla^s u$, that is, the inequality (H0).

Application in the mechanics of fluid#

In fluid dynamics, we seeks to describe the evolution of a velocity field $u = u(x,t)$, which represents the motion of a fluid. The fundamental equations governing viscous flows, such as the Navier-Stokes equations, combine transport effects with internal forces generated by viscosity. For Newtonian fluids, these internal forces re described by the viscous stress tensor, defined again by (1). In this context, $\mu > 0$ is called the dynamic viscosity, while $\lambda > 0$ is called the bulk viscosity. As in elasticity, the stress depends only on the symmetric part $\nabla^s u$ of the gradient. The rate of energy dissipation due to viscosity is given by (2). This implies that the dissipation provides control only on $\nabla^su$ according to (3). Therefore, we are led to look for an inequality (H0) to get the regularity and stability of the velocity field $u$.

Korn-type inequalities#

Unfortunately, the inequality (H0) is not true for all the vector fields. Consider a rigid motion

$$ u(x) = Ax + b, $$

where $A \in \mathfrak{M}^a$ is an antisymmetric constant matrix and $b \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a constant vector. The gradient $\nabla u$ of u is equal to $A$, while its symmetric part $\nabla^s u$ vanishes. As a consequence, the inequality (H0) can not hold for such rigid motions.

The key idea to eliminate this degeneracy, proposed by the mathematician Arthur Korn, is to either impose suitable boundary conditions that exclude rigid motions, or to work modulo rigid motions. We next briefly review Korn’s results and some variants.

Korn’s first inequality states that, there exists a constant $C_{K1}(\Omega) > 0$ such that for all vector fields $u : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$ with $u|_{\partial\Omega} = 0$ where $\partial\Omega$ is the boundary of $\Omega$, it holds

$$ \|\nabla u\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2\le C_{K1}(\Omega) \|\nabla^su\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. \tag{H1} $$

Instead of imposing boundary condition, Korn’s second inequality holds by work modulo rigid motions. Let $\mathcal{R}(\Omega)$ be the finite-dimensional set of all rigid motions on $\Omega$, there exists a constant $C_{K2}(\Omega) > 0$ such that for all vector fields $u:\Omega\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$,

$$ \inf\limits_{R\in\mathcal{R}(\Omega)}\|\nabla(u - R)\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le C_{K2}(\Omega)\|\nabla^su\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. \tag{H2} $$

Some well-known variants are the following:

$$ \|u\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \|\nabla u\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le C_K'(\Omega)\left(\|u\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2+\|\nabla^s u\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2\right). $$

and

$$ \inf\limits_{R\in\mathcal{R}(\Omega)}\|\nabla(u - R)\|_{L^q(\Omega)} \le C_K''(\Omega)\|\nabla^su\|_{L^p(\Omega)}, $$

for any $1 \le p < \infty$, $p \neq d$, $q = dp/(d-p)$ ($q = \infty$ if $p > d$).

Proof of Korn’s second inequality#

Let us here briefly recall an elegant and robust proof of (H2) based on an argument of Jacques-Louis Lions according to the statements in [3].

Sobolev spaces#

Before the proof, we shall recall fundamental knowledge of function spaces. The Sobolev space $H_0^1(\Omega)$ is defined as the closure of smooth compactly supported functions with respect to the $H^1$ norm, namely,

$$ f \in H_0^1(\Omega) \iff \exists f_n\in C_c^\infty(\Omega),\quad f_n \rightarrow f \;\text{in}\; H^1(\Omega). $$

If $\partial\Omega$ is regular enough, it can be interpreted as the set of functions that vanish on the boundary,

$$ H_0^1(\Omega) = \left\{f\in H^1(\Omega)\,;\, f|_{\partial\Omega} = 0 \right\}. $$

The space $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ is defined as the dual space of $H_0^1(\Omega)$, namely, the space of continuous linear functionals on $H_0^1(\Omega)$. The standard dual norm of $u \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$ reads

$$ \|f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} = \sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,;\, \varphi \in H_0^1(\Omega),\|\varphi\|_{H^1(\Omega)}\le 1\right\}. $$

The Poincaré inequality tells us that for all $f \in H_0^1(\Omega)$,

$$ \|\nabla f\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le \|f\|_{H^1(\Omega)}^2 \le (1+C_P^2)\|\nabla f\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

Since $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ is dense in $H_0^1(\Omega)$, an equivalent form of $H^{-1}$ norm is given by

$$ \|f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} = \sup\left\{|\int_{\Omega}f\varphi|\,;\, \varphi \in C_c^\infty(\Omega),\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^2(\Omega)}\le 1\right\}. $$

There is another very useful equivalent characterization of the $H^{-1}$ norm. For $f \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$, define $\phi$ as the solution in $H_0^1(\Omega)$ of the Laplace problem on $\Omega$, i.e.,

$$ -\Delta\phi = f, \quad \phi|_{\partial\Omega} = 0. $$

By a standard Lax-Milgram argument, there exists a bijective operator $\Delta^{-1}$ that maps $ f \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$ to $\phi \in H_0^1(\Omega)$. Using integration by parts, we can verify the following proposition.

Proposition 1. The $H^{-1}$ norm of $f$ is equivalent to the $L^2$ norm of $\nabla\Delta^{-1}f$.

The Lions lemma [4, Theorem 3.2] states that

$$ L^2(\Omega) = \left\{f \in H^{-1}(\Omega)\,;\, \nabla f \in [H^{-1}(\Omega)]^d\right\}. $$

Moreover, Duvant and Lions have shown the following inequality in [5, Remark 3.1]:

$$ \|f\|_{L^2(\Omega)} \le C\left(\|f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} + \|\nabla f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\right), $$

where

$$ \|\nabla f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^d\|\partial_i f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/2}. $$

Poincaré–Lions inquality#

We introduce the notation

$$ \langle f\rangle = \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}f, $$

where $|\Omega|$ stands for the volume of $\Omega$. In [3], Desvillettes and Villani present a strong lemma that involves a Poincaré-type inequality with respect to the $H^{-1}$ norm of $\nabla f$. We will review this lemma and its proof here.

Lemma 2. There exists a constant $C(\Omega)$ such that for all $f \in L^2(\Omega)$,

$$ \|\nabla f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 \le d\|f - \langle f\rangle\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le C(\Omega)\|\nabla f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2. $$

Proof of Lemma 2. Regarding the first inequality, for each $f \in L^2(\Omega)$, we have $\partial_i f \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$ and then by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality

$$ (\partial_i f)(\varphi) = -\int_{\Omega}f \partial_i \varphi \le \|f\|_{L^2(\Omega)}\|\partial_i\varphi\|_{L^2(\Omega)},\quad \forall \varphi \in C_c^\infty(\Omega). $$

This implies that for all $1 \le i \le d$,

$$ \|\partial_i f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} \le \|f\|_{L^2(\Omega)}. $$

Then, for each $f \in L^2(\Omega)$, we have $f - \langle f\rangle \in L^2(\Omega)$ due to the boundedness of $\Omega$ and

$$ \|\nabla f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{d}\|\partial_i f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{d}\|\partial_i (f-\langle f\rangle)\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2\le d\|f-\langle f\rangle\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

For the second inequality, we present a proof based on the closed graph theorem. We denote by $X$ the set of all functions in $L^2(\Omega)$ of which the mean is zero, namely,

$$ X = \left\{f \in L^2(\Omega)\,;\, \langle f\rangle = 0\right\}, $$

and $Y = [H^{-1}(\Omega)]^d$. Define the linear operator $T: X \rightarrow Y$ by $(Tf)_i = \partial_i f$ for all $1 \le i \le d$. First, $T$ is injective on $X$. Indeed, let $f \in X$ and $Tf = 0$, then by definition $f$ is a constant. Since $\langle f\rangle = 0$, we have $f = 0$. Second, we denote by $Z$ the range of $T$ and define the inverse $T^{-1}: Z \subset Y \rightarrow X$ by $T^{-1}(\nabla f) = f$. For a sequence $(g_n) = (\nabla f_n) \subset Z $ that converges to $g \in Y$ and another sequence $(T^{-1}(g_n)) = (f_n) \subset X$ that converges to $f \in X$, we want to show that $g = \nabla f$. Indeed, for every test function $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ and $ 1 \le i \le d$, we have

$$ g_{n,i}(\varphi) = (\partial_i f_n)(\varphi) = -\int_{\Omega} f_n\partial_i\varphi. $$

Taking the limit $n \rightarrow +\infty$ gives

$$ g_i(\varphi) = - \lim\limits_{n\rightarrow +\infty}\int_{\Omega} f_n\partial_i\varphi = - \int_{\Omega} (\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow +\infty}f_n)\partial_i\varphi = - \int_{\Omega} f\partial_i\varphi = (\partial_i f)(\varphi). $$

This implies $g = \nabla f$ and hence the graph of $T^{-1}$ is closed in $Z \times X$. By the closed graph theorem, $T^{-1}$ is bounded. Therefore, there exists a constant $C(\Omega) > 0$ such that for all $g \in Z$,

$$ \|T^{-1}g\|_{L^2(\Omega)} \le C(\Omega)\|g\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}. $$

Since $T^{-1}$ is surjective on $X$, we deduce that for all $f \in L^2(\Omega)$,

$$ \|f - \langle f\rangle\|_{L^2(\Omega)} \le C(\Omega)\|\nabla f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}. $$$$ \phantom{.}\tag*{$\square$} $$

Remark 1. Desvillettes and Villani also mentioned another simple and constructive proof provided by Meyer for the second inquality in Lemma 2. We refer to [3] for more details.

Schwarz theorem#

The following lemma allows us to write all the components of the second-order differential of a vector field $u$ in terms of its symmetric part of the gradient.

Lemma 3. Let $u : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$ be a vector field in $[H^1(\Omega)]^d$. Then, for all $1\le i,j,k\le d$,

$$ \partial_k(\nabla^a u)_{ij} = \partial_j(\nabla^su)_{ik} - \partial_i(\nabla^su)_{jk}. $$

This algebraic property is at the core of all Korn’s type inequalities. It implies that the derivatives of $\nabla^au$ are linear combinations of the derivatives of $\nabla^su$.

The optimal rigid motion#

Lemma 4. Let $u : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$ be a vector field in $[H^1(\Omega)]^d$, then

$$ \|\nabla u-\langle\nabla^au\rangle\|_{L^2(\Omega)} = \inf\limits_{R\in\mathcal{R}(\Omega)} \|\nabla(u-R)\|_{L^2(\Omega)}. $$

Proof of Lemma 4. For any rigid motion $R(x) = Ax + b$ with $A \in \mathfrak{M}^a$, we have

$$ \|\nabla(u - R)\|_{L^2(\Omega)} = \|\nabla u - A\|_{L^2(\Omega)} = \|\nabla^s u + \nabla^a u - A\|_{L^2(\Omega)}. $$

Since the symmetric matrix and antiasymmetric matrices are orthogonal in the Frobenius product, we get

$$ \|\nabla(u - R)\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 = \|\nabla^s u\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \|\nabla^a u - A\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

Therefore, the minimization reduces to

$$ \inf\limits_{A\in\mathfrak{M}^a}\|\nabla^au - A\|_{L^2(\Omega)}. $$

This is just the best approximation of $\nabla^au$ by a constant antisymmetric matrix. The minimier is its mean value:

$$ A_* = \langle\nabla^au\rangle = \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\nabla^au. $$

Indeed, for any $A \in \mathfrak{M}^a$,

$$ \nabla^a u - A = (\nabla^au - \langle\nabla^au\rangle) + (\langle\nabla^au\rangle - A). $$

The first term has zero mean, while the second is constant. Hence they are orthogonal in $L^2(\Omega)$, and

$$ \|\nabla^au - A\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 = \|\nabla^au - \langle\nabla^au\rangle\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + |\Omega||\langle\nabla^au\rangle - A|^2. $$

The minimum is attained when $A = \langle \nabla^au\rangle$, which completes the proof.

$$ \phantom{.}\tag*{$\square$} $$

Main proof#

Now we turn to the proof of the Korn’s second inequlity (H2) based on the above preliminary results. By the Lemma 4, (H2) is equivalent to

$$ \|\nabla^a u - \langle\nabla^au\rangle\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le C_{K2}\|\nabla^su\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

By the second inequality in Lemma 2, we have

$$ \|\nabla^a u - \langle\nabla^au\rangle\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le C\|\nabla\nabla^a u\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

By the Lemma 3, $\nabla\nabla^au$ is a matrix combination of $\nabla\nabla^su$. Hence, using the first inequality in Lemma 2 and the triangle inequality, we derive the inequality (H2).

$$ \phantom{.}\tag*{$\square$} $$
