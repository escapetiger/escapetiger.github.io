Abstract. In elasticity and viscous-fluid mechanics, energy controls the symmetric part of a vector-field gradient, whereas compactness and well-posedness usually require control of the full gradient. Korn’s inequality bridges this gap after the invisible rigid motions have been removed. This post explains the geometric obstruction, states the principal bounded-domain estimates, and gives a detailed proof of the quotient form using the Poincaré–Lions inequality in $H^{-1}$.

1. The question behind Korn’s inequality#

Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, $d \ge 2$, be a bounded, connected domain with $C^1$ boundary. For a vector field $u : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^d$, write

$$ \nabla u = \varepsilon(u) + \omega(u),\qquad \varepsilon(u) := \frac{\nabla u + \nabla u^T}{2},\qquad \omega(u) := \frac{\nabla u - \nabla u^T}{2}. $$

The matrix $\varepsilon(u)$ is the symmetric gradient or linearized strain, while $\omega(u)$ is the skew-symmetric gradient or infinitesimal rotation. Pointwise, the two matrices are orthogonal in the Frobenius inner product:

$$ \varepsilon(u) : \omega(u) = 0,\qquad \lvert \nabla u \rvert_F^2 = \lvert \varepsilon(u) \rvert_F^2 + \lvert \omega(u) \rvert_F^2. $$

The central question is therefore:

When does control of $\varepsilon(u)$ imply control of the entire gradient $\nabla u$?

Without an additional condition, the answer is never, because the symmetric gradient cannot detect rigid motions. Korn’s inequality says that this is the only obstruction: after boundary conditions or a suitable projection remove rigid motions, the symmetric gradient controls the full gradient.

The inequality originates in the elasticity work of Korn (1908). A systematic functional-analytic treatment appears in Duvaut and Lions (1976, Chapter III, Section 3.3), while the concise $H^{-1}$-based proof strategy followed below appears in Desvillettes and Villani (2002, Section 1).

2. Notation and functional setting#

For $u = (u_1, \ldots, u_d) \in H^1(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$, our convention is

$$ (\nabla u)_{ij} = \partial_j u_i,\qquad \varepsilon_{ij}(u) = \frac{\partial_j u_i + \partial_i u_j}{2},\qquad \omega_{ij}(u) = \frac{\partial_j u_i - \partial_i u_j}{2}. $$

For matrices $M, N \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, set

$$ M : N := \operatorname{tr}(M^T N) = \sum_{i, j = 1}^d M_{ij}N_{ij}, \qquad \lvert M \rvert_F := (M : M)^{1/2}. $$

The corresponding $L^2$ norm is

$$ \lVert M \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 := \int_\Omega \lvert M(x) \rvert_F^2 \,\mathrm{d}x. $$

For an integrable scalar-, vector-, or matrix-valued function $f$, its mean is denoted by

$$ \langle f \rangle_\Omega := \frac{1}{\lvert \Omega \rvert}\int_\Omega f(x) \,\mathrm{d}x. $$

The space of infinitesimal rigid motions is

$$ \mathcal{R}(\Omega) := \left\lbrace R : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^d \,\middle\vert\, R(x) = Ax + b,\ A^T = -A,\ b \in \mathbb{R}^d \right\rbrace. $$

Translations have zero gradient, while infinitesimal rotations have a constant skew-symmetric gradient.

3. Why mechanics controls only the symmetric gradient#

3.1 Linear elasticity#

A displacement field $u$ sends the reference point $x$ to the deformed position

$$ y(x) = x + u(x). $$

Its deformation gradient is $I + \nabla u$. The induced change of the Euclidean metric is measured by

$$ (\nabla y)^T \nabla y - I = (I + \nabla u)^T(I + \nabla u) - I = 2\varepsilon(u) + (\nabla u)^T \nabla u. $$

In the small-displacement regime, the quadratic term $(\nabla u)^T\nabla u$ is neglected. Thus the first-order change of lengths and angles is encoded by $\varepsilon(u)$, not by $\omega(u)$.

For a homogeneous isotropic linearly elastic material, the constitutive law is

$$ \sigma(u) = 2\mu\,\varepsilon(u) + \lambda\,\operatorname{tr}(\varepsilon(u))I, $$

and the stored-energy density is

$$ W(\varepsilon(u)) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma(u) : \varepsilon(u) = \mu\lvert \varepsilon(u) \rvert_F^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}\varepsilon(u)\bigr)^2. $$

The standard stability conditions

$$ \mu > 0,\qquad 2\mu + d\lambda > 0 $$

make this quadratic form positive definite on symmetric matrices. Consequently, for some material constant $c_{\mathrm{el}} > 0$,

$$ W(E) \ge c_{\mathrm{el}}\lvert E \rvert_F^2\qquad\text{for every symmetric matrix }E. $$

The elastic energy therefore controls $\varepsilon(u)$. Korn’s inequality is what converts that control into an $H^1$ estimate for $u$, once rigid motions have been excluded. This is one reason the inequality is foundational in the variational theory of elasticity (Duvaut and Lions, 1976).

3.2 Incompressible Newtonian fluids#

For an incompressible Newtonian fluid with dynamic viscosity $\mu > 0$, the viscous part of the stress is

$$ \tau(u) = 2\mu\,\varepsilon(u). $$

Because symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices are orthogonal,

$$ \tau(u) : \nabla u = 2\mu\,\varepsilon(u) : \bigl(\varepsilon(u) + \omega(u)\bigr) = 2\mu\lvert \varepsilon(u) \rvert_F^2. $$

Thus viscous dissipation also sees only the symmetric gradient. Depending on the boundary conditions, a Korn estimate turns this dissipation into control of $\nabla u$. The same structural issue therefore appears in both elasticity and hydrodynamics (Desvillettes and Villani, 2002).

4. The obstruction: rigid motions#

Take

$$ R(x) = Ax + b,\qquad A^T = -A. $$

Then

$$ \nabla R = A,\qquad \varepsilon(R) = \frac{A + A^T}{2} = 0. $$

If $A \neq 0$, the full gradient is nonzero although the symmetric gradient vanishes. Hence no estimate of the form

$$ \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)} \le C\lVert \varepsilon(u) \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)} $$

can hold for every $u \in H^1(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$.

Rigid motions are not merely examples in the kernel; they are the whole kernel.

Proposition (kernel of the symmetric gradient). If $\Omega$ is connected and $u \in H^1(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$, then $\varepsilon(u) = 0$ almost everywhere if and only if $u \in \mathcal{R}(\Omega)$.

The nontrivial direction follows from the distributional identity

$$ \partial_k\omega_{ij}(u) = \partial_j\varepsilon_{ik}(u) - \partial_i\varepsilon_{jk}(u). \tag{4.1} $$

If $\varepsilon(u) = 0$, then every weak derivative of every component of $\omega(u)$ vanishes. Connectedness implies that $\omega(u)$ is a constant skew-symmetric matrix $A$. Since $\nabla u = \omega(u) = A$, the vector field $u - Ax$ has zero weak gradient and is therefore a constant vector $b$. Hence $u(x) = Ax + b$.

This calculation already reveals the strategy of Korn’s inequality: control the variation of $\omega(u)$ by derivatives of $\varepsilon(u)$, and then remove the constant part of $\omega(u)$.

5. Three standard forms#

The labels “first Korn inequality” and “second Korn inequality” are not completely uniform across the literature. It is safer to identify each estimate by its normalization; compare Desvillettes and Villani (2002, equations (1)–(3)).

5.1 Homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition#

Boundary Korn inequality. For every $u \in H_0^1(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$,

$$ \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le 2\lVert \varepsilon(u) \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. \tag{5.1} $$

This version has a short proof. For $u \in C_c^\infty(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$, expansion and integration by parts give

$$ 2\lVert \varepsilon(u) \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 = \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \sum_{i, j = 1}^d\int_\Omega \partial_j u_i\,\partial_i u_j \,\mathrm{d}x = \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \lVert \operatorname{div}u \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

Dropping the nonnegative divergence term proves (5.1) for smooth compactly supported fields. Density of $C_c^\infty(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$ in $H_0^1(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$ gives the general result.

The zero trace removes every nonzero rigid motion, so no quotient is needed.

5.2 A lower-order stabilization#

Stabilized Korn inequality. There exists $C = C(\Omega) > 0$ such that every $u \in H^1(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$ satisfies

$$ \lVert u \rVert_{H^1(\Omega)} \le C\left(\lVert u \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)} + \lVert \varepsilon(u) \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}\right). \tag{5.2} $$

The $L^2$ term prevents an uncontrolled rigid motion from violating the estimate. This form is particularly useful in compactness arguments.

5.3 Working modulo rigid motions#

Korn inequality modulo rigid motions. There exists $C_K = C_K(\Omega) > 0$ such that every $u \in H^1(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$ satisfies

$$ \inf_{R \in \mathcal{R}(\Omega)}\lVert \nabla(u - R) \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)} \le C_K\lVert \varepsilon(u) \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}. \tag{5.3} $$

Equivalently, after also choosing the translational part of $R$,

$$ \inf_{R \in \mathcal{R}(\Omega)}\lVert u - R \rVert_{H^1(\Omega)} \le C_K'\lVert \varepsilon(u) \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}. \tag{5.4} $$

Estimate (5.3) is the version we shall prove. It expresses the precise geometric content of Korn’s inequality: $\varepsilon(u)$ controls the distance from $u$ to the finite-dimensional space of rigid motions.

6. The functional-analytic engine: a Poincaré–Lions estimate#

6.1 The $H^{-1}$ norm#

Let $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ be the dual of $H_0^1(\Omega)$. We use the equivalent norm

$$ \lVert f \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} := \sup\left\lbrace \lvert \langle f, \varphi \rangle \rvert \,\middle\vert\, \varphi \in C_c^\infty(\Omega),\ \lVert \nabla\varphi \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)} \le 1 \right\rbrace. $$

The Poincaré inequality on $H_0^1(\Omega)$ makes this norm equivalent to the usual dual norm based on $\lVert \varphi \rVert_{H^1}$.

For $g \in L^2(\Omega)$, distributional differentiation gives

$$ \langle \partial_i g, \varphi \rangle = -\int_\Omega g\,\partial_i\varphi \,\mathrm{d}x. $$

Therefore,

$$ \lVert \partial_i g \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} \le \lVert g \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}. \tag{6.1} $$

For a vector distribution $F = (F_1, \ldots, F_d)$, define

$$ \lVert F \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 := \sum_{i = 1}^d\lVert F_i \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2. $$

6.2 Lions’ regularity estimate#

The following distributional result is the key analytic input.

Lions’ lemma. If $f \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$ and $\partial_i f \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, d$, then $f \in L^2(\Omega)$. Moreover,

$$ \lVert f \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)} \le C_L(\Omega)\left(\lVert f \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} + \lVert \nabla f \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\right). \tag{6.2} $$

This is Duvaut and Lions (1976, Chapter III, Theorem 3.2, p. 111, and Remark 3.1, p. 112). It is a regularity theorem for distributions: first-order information measured in a negative Sobolev norm recovers an $L^2$ estimate.

6.3 Poincaré–Lions inequality#

Lemma (Poincaré–Lions). There exists $C_{\mathrm{PL}} = C_{\mathrm{PL}}(\Omega) > 0$ such that every $f \in L^2(\Omega)$ satisfies

$$ \lVert \nabla f \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 \le d\lVert f - \langle f \rangle_\Omega \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le C_{\mathrm{PL}}\lVert \nabla f \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2. \tag{6.3} $$

This formulation is used explicitly by Desvillettes and Villani (2002, Lemma 2).

Proof. Set $g = f - \langle f \rangle_\Omega$. Because $\partial_i f = \partial_i g$, estimate (6.1) gives

$$ \lVert \nabla f \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 = \sum_{i = 1}^d\lVert \partial_i g \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 \le d\lVert g \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

The reverse estimate is the difficult part. Suppose it were false. Then there would exist $f_n \in L^2(\Omega)$ such that

$$ \langle f_n \rangle_\Omega = 0,\qquad \lVert f_n \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)} = 1,\qquad \lVert \nabla f_n \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} \longrightarrow 0. $$

After passing to a subsequence, $f_n \rightharpoonup f$ weakly in $L^2(\Omega)$. Since $\Omega$ is bounded, the embedding $L^2(\Omega) \hookrightarrow H^{-1}(\Omega)$ is compact; this is the dual counterpart of the compact embedding $H_0^1(\Omega) \hookrightarrow L^2(\Omega)$. Hence

$$ f_n \longrightarrow f\qquad\text{strongly in }H^{-1}(\Omega). $$

The distributional limit of $\nabla f_n$ is both $\nabla f$ and zero, so $\nabla f = 0$. Connectedness of $\Omega$ implies that $f$ is constant. Weak $L^2$ convergence preserves the zero-mean condition, and therefore $f = 0$. We have proved that $f_n \to 0$ in $H^{-1}(\Omega)$. Applying (6.2) now gives

$$ 1 = \lVert f_n \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)} \le C_L(\Omega)\left(\lVert f_n \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)} + \lVert \nabla f_n \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\right) \longrightarrow 0, $$

a contradiction. Thus the reverse inequality holds. $\square$

7. Proof of Korn’s inequality modulo rigid motions#

The proof has two steps. First we control the nonconstant part of the infinitesimal rotation. Then we identify the constant rotation that minimizes the distance to $\nabla u$.

7.1 A derivative identity#

Let

$$ E := \varepsilon(u),\qquad W := \omega(u). $$

For every $i, j, k \in \left\lbrace 1, \ldots, d \right\rbrace$, commutation of distributional derivatives gives

$$ \partial_k W_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_k\partial_j u_i - \partial_k\partial_i u_j\right) = \partial_j E_{ik} - \partial_i E_{jk}. \tag{7.1} $$

This identity is sometimes described as the Schwarz-theorem step in the proof. It is the algebraic core of the argument: every derivative of the skew part is a linear combination of derivatives of the symmetric part. The same identity is highlighted by Carrapatoso et al. (2022, equation (23)).

7.2 Control of the varying rotation#

Apply the reverse inequality in (6.3) to each scalar component $W_{ij}$:

$$ \lVert W - \langle W \rangle_\Omega \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le C_{\mathrm{PL}}\sum_{i, j, k = 1}^d\lVert \partial_k W_{ij} \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2. \tag{7.2} $$

By (7.1), the triangle inequality, and (6.1),

$$ \lVert \partial_k W_{ij} \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 \le 2\lVert \partial_j E_{ik} \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 + 2\lVert \partial_i E_{jk} \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 \le 2\lVert E_{ik} \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + 2\lVert E_{jk} \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

Summing over $i, j, k$ yields

$$ \sum_{i, j, k = 1}^d\lVert \partial_k W_{ij} \rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^2 \le 4d\lVert E \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

Consequently,

$$ \lVert W - \langle W \rangle_\Omega \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le 4dC_{\mathrm{PL}}\lVert E \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. \tag{7.3} $$

The $H^{-1}$ norm is essential here. The derivatives of $E$ need not lie in $L^2$ when $u \in H^1$, but they always belong to $H^{-1}$.

7.3 The optimal rigid rotation#

Let $A$ be any constant skew-symmetric matrix. Since $E$ is symmetric and $W - A$ is skew-symmetric,

$$ \lVert \nabla u - A \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 = \lVert E \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \lVert W - A \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. \tag{7.4} $$

The mean $\langle W\rangle_\Omega$ is itself skew-symmetric, and the orthogonal decomposition into a zero-mean part and a constant part gives

$$ \lVert W - A \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 = \lVert W - \langle W \rangle_\Omega \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \lvert \Omega \rvert\,\lvert \langle W \rangle_\Omega - A \rvert_F^2. \tag{7.5} $$

Therefore the unique minimizing matrix is

$$ A_* = \langle \omega(u) \rangle_\Omega. \tag{7.6} $$

Combining (7.3)–(7.6), we obtain

$$ \inf_{A^T = -A}\lVert \nabla u - A \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \le \left(1 + 4dC_{\mathrm{PL}}\right)\lVert \varepsilon(u) \rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. $$

Because $\nabla(Ax + b) = A$, the translational vector $b$ does not affect this gradient minimization. This proves (5.3).

To obtain (5.4), choose $A = A_*$ and then set

$$ b_* := \langle u - A_*x \rangle_\Omega. $$

The field $u - (A_x + b_)$ has zero mean, so the usual Poincaré inequality controls its $L^2$ norm by $\lVert \nabla u - A_* \rVert_{L^2}$. This adds the missing zeroth-order control and completes the $H^1$ quotient estimate. $\square$

8. What changes under other constraints?#

8.1 Tangential boundary conditions and axisymmetry#

A boundary condition need not eliminate every rigid motion. For example, the tangency condition

$$ u \cdot n = 0\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega $$

may still admit a rigid rotation tangent to the boundary. This happens on axisymmetric domains. Desvillettes and Villani prove that the corresponding Korn estimate holds without subtracting a rigid rotation precisely when the geometry excludes such a nonzero tangent rigid motion; in the axisymmetric case, one must project out the surviving rotational mode. Their quantitative analysis was motivated by kinetic theory rather than elasticity (Desvillettes and Villani, 2002).

The lesson is general: a boundary condition is useful only to the extent that it removes the kernel compatible with that condition.

8.2 Weighted inequalities on the whole space#

On $\mathbb{R}^d$, unweighted Poincaré and Korn estimates cannot simply be copied from a bounded domain. A confining measure

$$ \mathrm{d}\mu(x) = Z^{-1} e^{-\phi(x)} \,\mathrm{d}x $$

can supply the missing coercivity. Under explicit normalization, regularity, and Poincaré assumptions on $\phi$, Carrapatoso et al. (2022) establish weighted Korn and Poincaré–Korn inequalities after projecting onto the appropriate finite-dimensional rigid modes. They also connect these estimates with self-adjoint operators built from the Witten Laplacian.

Their argument retains the same architecture as the bounded-domain proof:

  1. isolate the finite-dimensional symmetry modes;
  2. use a weighted Poincaré or Poincaré–Lions inequality;
  3. invoke the derivative identity (7.1) to replace derivatives of the skew part by derivatives of the symmetric part.

Thus the elementary bounded-domain proof is not an isolated trick. It is the prototype of a broader coercivity mechanism used in kinetic theory and hypocoercivity.

9. Common misconceptions#

  • The symmetric gradient does not determine the displacement without a normalization. Translations are invisible to $\nabla u$, and infinitesimal rotations are invisible to $\varepsilon(u)$.

  • Korn’s inequality does not say that the skew part is small pointwise. It says that, after subtracting one constant skew matrix, its $L^2$ norm is controlled by the symmetric part.

  • The domain hypothesis matters. Connectedness identifies zero-gradient functions with a single constant, while boundary regularity supports the compactness and localization results used in the Poincaré–Lions estimate.

  • The Korn constant is geometric. Under a tangential boundary condition, it can deteriorate as the domain approaches axisymmetry; Desvillettes and Villani (2002) quantify this dependence on the shape of $\Omega$.

  • Names alone are unreliable. Different sources attach “first” and “second” to different normalizations. Always inspect the boundary conditions, the lower-order term, and the rigid-motion quotient.

10. Final perspective#

Korn’s inequality is a coercivity theorem with a geometric kernel. The energy-relevant tensor $\varepsilon(u)$ forgets infinitesimal rigid motions, but it forgets nothing else. The proof makes this precise through a clean chain:

$$ \varepsilon(u) \xrightarrow{\text{differentiate}} \nabla\omega(u) \xrightarrow{\text{Poincaré--Lions}} \omega(u) - \langle \omega(u) \rangle_\Omega \xrightarrow{\text{orthogonal projection}} \nabla u - \langle \omega(u) \rangle_\Omega. $$

Each arrow has a distinct role. The derivative identity supplies the algebra, the $H^{-1}$ estimate avoids demanding second-order regularity, and the projection removes the finite-dimensional symmetry. This combination explains why Korn’s inequality is both a basic tool of continuum mechanics and a reusable template for modern weighted and kinetic coercivity estimates.
