Abstract. A smooth manifold tells us what it means to move and differentiate, but it does not determine a dynamics. A Poisson structure adds an antisymmetric bracket on smooth functions that turns each function into a vector field and each Hamiltonian into an evolution law. This post introduces that idea from canonical Hamiltonian mechanics, passes from the algebraic bracket to the intrinsic Poisson bivector, explains the Jacobi identity and its coordinate form, and shows how a possibly degenerate Poisson manifold decomposes into symplectic leaves. The main examples are canonical phase space, two-dimensional Poisson structures, and the Lie–Poisson structure on $\mathbb{R}^3$.

A smooth manifold provides a stage on which calculus can be performed. At a point $p \in M$, it supplies a tangent space $T_pM$, a cotangent space $T_p^*M$, and smooth functions whose differentials belong to those cotangent spaces. But the manifold alone does not tell us how a point should move, which quantities should be conserved, or how two observables should interact.

A Poisson structure supplies precisely this additional layer. Its central object is a bracket

$$ \lbrace \cdot, \cdot \rbrace : C^\infty(M) \times C^\infty(M) \longrightarrow C^\infty(M), $$

which converts smooth functions into infinitesimal motions. Once a Hamiltonian function $H$ is chosen, the bracket determines a vector field $X_H$ and therefore a differential equation. Unlike a symplectic form, however, a Poisson structure is allowed to be degenerate. Its effective phase-space dimension may vary from point to point.

The guiding principle of this article is therefore:

$$ \boxed{\text{A Poisson manifold is a smooth manifold foliated by symplectic phase spaces.}} $$

This sentence is only a preview. To understand it properly, we must explain the bracket, its Jacobi identity, the bivector hidden behind it, and the symplectic leaves on which the dynamics actually takes place.

Throughout, manifolds are finite-dimensional smooth real manifolds, Hausdorff and second countable, without boundary unless stated otherwise. Functions, vector fields, and tensor fields are smooth, and all functions are real-valued. The sign convention for Hamiltonian vector fields is fixed in Section 1 and used consistently thereafter.

1. From phase space to the Poisson bracket#

Consider the canonical phase space $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ with coordinates

$$ (q^1, \ldots, q^n, p_1, \ldots, p_n), $$

where $q^i$ denotes a position coordinate and $p_i$ its conjugate momentum. Given a Hamiltonian $H \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^{2n})$, Hamilton’s equations are

$$ \dot q^i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n. $$

These equations define the Hamiltonian vector field

$$ X_H = \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^i} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}\right). $$

If $f \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^{2n})$ is an observable and $\gamma(t)$ is a trajectory satisfying $\dot\gamma(t) = X_H\vert_{\gamma(t)}$, then the chain rule gives

$$ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f(\gamma(t)) = X_H(f)(\gamma(t)). $$

The right-hand side can be written using the canonical Poisson bracket

$$ \lbrace f, g \rbrace = \sum_{i = 1}^n \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q^i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial q^i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}\right). \tag{1.1} $$

With this sign convention,

$$ X_H(f) = \lbrace H, f \rbrace, $$

so Hamilton’s equations become

$$ \dot q^i = \lbrace H, q^i \rbrace, \quad \dot p_i = \lbrace H, p_i \rbrace. $$

Some texts use the negative bracket and consequently write

$$ \dot{f} = \lbrace f, H \rbrace. $$

Both conventions describe the same geometry, but one must not switch between them in the middle of a calculation.

The bracket immediately expresses conservation laws. An observable $f$ is constant along the Hamiltonian flow precisely when

$$ \lbrace H, f \rbrace = 0. $$

In particular, the Hamiltonian is conserved because the bracket is antisymmetric:

$$ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}H(\gamma(t)) = \lbrace H, H \rbrace = 0. $$

Historically, the bracket arose in Poisson’s study of mechanics, while the identity now bearing Jacobi’s name explains why the bracket of two conserved quantities is again conserved; a concise modern account is given by Bursztyn (2025). The modern differential-geometric theory was shaped in particular by Lichnerowicz (1977) and Weinstein (1983). The decisive conceptual step is to retain the structural properties of (1.1) without insisting on canonical coordinates or even on an even-dimensional ambient manifold.

2. The definition of a Poisson manifold#

Let $M$ be a smooth manifold, and let $C^\infty(M)$ denote the real vector space of smooth functions $M \to \mathbb{R}$.

Definition (Poisson bracket). A Poisson bracket on $M$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear map $\lbrace \cdot, \cdot \rbrace : C^\infty(M) \times C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ such that, for all $f, g, h \in C^\infty(M)$, the following three properties hold:

  1. Skew-symmetry: $\lbrace f, g \rbrace = -\lbrace g, f \rbrace$.
  2. Jacobi identity: $\lbrace f, \lbrace g, h \rbrace \rbrace + \lbrace g, \lbrace h, f \rbrace \rbrace + \lbrace h, \lbrace f, g \rbrace \rbrace = 0$.
  3. Leibniz rule: $\lbrace f, gh \rbrace = \lbrace f, g \rbrace h + g \lbrace f, h \rbrace$.

Definition (Poisson manifold). A Poisson manifold is a pair $(M, \lbrace \cdot, \cdot \rbrace)$ consisting of a smooth manifold $M$ and a Poisson bracket on $C^\infty(M)$.

This is the standard smooth definition used, for example, by Bursztyn (2025, Section 2.1) and Crainic et al. (2021).

The three axioms have different jobs.

Skew-symmetry and the Jacobi identity say that $C^\infty(M)$ is a Lie algebra under $\lbrace \cdot, \cdot \rbrace$. The Leibniz rule says that the bracket is compatible with the ordinary pointwise product of functions. Thus $C^\infty(M)$ simultaneously carries a commutative multiplication and a Lie bracket, tied together by a first-order differentiation rule. This combined object is called a Poisson algebra.

Because of skew-symmetry, the Leibniz rule also holds in the first argument. Indeed,

$$ \lbrace fg, h \rbrace = -\lbrace h, fg \rbrace = -\lbrace h, f \rbrace g - f \lbrace h, g \rbrace = f \lbrace g, h \rbrace + g \lbrace f, h \rbrace. $$

Therefore the bracket is a derivation in each argument separately.

2.1 Why the Leibniz rule matters#

Fix $f \in C^\infty(M)$. The map

$$ D_f : C^\infty(M) \longrightarrow C^\infty(M), \qquad D_f(g) = \lbrace f, g \rbrace, $$

is a derivation. A fundamental fact from differential geometry says that derivations of $C^\infty(M)$ are exactly smooth vector fields. Consequently, there is a unique vector field $X_f \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ satisfying

$$ X_f(g) = \lbrace f, g \rbrace \qquad\text{for every }g \in C^\infty(M). \tag{2.1} $$

This is the Hamiltonian vector field generated by $f$.

The Leibniz rule is therefore what converts an algebraic operation on functions into a geometric direction of motion. Without it, $g \mapsto \lbrace f, g \rbrace$ need not be a vector field and the bracket would not define a local dynamics on $M$.

2.2 Why the Jacobi identity matters#

The Jacobi identity is not a decorative algebraic condition. Applying (2.1) to a test function $h$ gives

$$ \begin{aligned} [X_f, X_g](h) &= X_f(X_g(h)) - X_g(X_f(h))\\ &= \lbrace f, \lbrace g, h \rbrace \rbrace - \lbrace g, \lbrace f, h \rbrace \rbrace \\ &= \lbrace \lbrace f, g \rbrace, h \rbrace \\ &= X_{\lbrace f, g \rbrace}(h). \end{aligned} $$

Since this holds for every $h$, we obtain

$$ \boxed{[X_f, X_g] = X_{\lbrace f, g \rbrace}.} \tag{2.2} $$

Thus the assignment $f \mapsto X_f$ preserves Lie brackets. Equation (2.2) has several consequences:

  • Hamiltonian vector fields are closed under commutators.
  • Hamiltonian flows preserve the Poisson structure.
  • If $f$ and $g$ are conserved by $H$, then $\lbrace f, g \rbrace$ is also conserved, because the Jacobi identity gives $\lbrace H, \lbrace f, g \rbrace \rbrace = 0$.

If skew-symmetry and the Leibniz rule hold but the Jacobi identity fails, one has an almost-Poisson bracket. Such a bracket still produces vector fields, but their commutators need not correspond to the bracket of their Hamiltonians. This failure is precisely the obstruction measured by the Jacobi identity.

3. From a bracket to a bivector field#

The definition above is algebraic: it speaks about pairs of smooth functions. Differential geometry offers an equivalent tensorial description.

At a point $p \in M$, the value $\lbrace f, g \rbrace(p)$ depends only on the differentials $\mathrm{d}f\vert_p$ and $\mathrm{d}g\vert_p$. One way to see this is to fix one argument and use the fact that a derivation at $p$ is determined by a tangent vector at $p$. Bilinearity and the derivation property in both arguments then imply the existence of a unique alternating bilinear form on $T_p^*M$ whose value on $\mathrm{d}f\vert_p$ and $\mathrm{d}g\vert_p$ is $\lbrace f, g \rbrace(p)$.

As $p$ varies, these alternating bilinear forms fit together smoothly.

Definition (Poisson bivector). The bivector field associated with a Poisson bracket is the section $\pi \in \Gamma(\wedge^2 TM)$ determined by

$$ \boxed{\lbrace f, g \rbrace = \pi(\mathrm{d}f, \mathrm{d}g).} \tag{3.1} $$

A bivector is the contravariant counterpart of a differential $2$-form. A $2$-form eats two tangent vectors, whereas a bivector eats two covectors:

$$ \omega_p : T_pM \times T_pM \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad \pi_p : T_p^*M \times T_p^*M \to \mathbb{R}. $$

The bivector defines a bundle map

$$ \pi^\sharp : T^*M \longrightarrow TM $$

by the rule

$$ \beta\bigl(\pi^\sharp(\alpha)\bigr) = \pi(\alpha, \beta), \qquad \alpha, \beta \in T_p^*M. \tag{3.2} $$

Comparing (3.1), (3.2), and (2.1), we find

$$ \boxed{X_f = \pi^\sharp(\mathrm{d}f).} \tag{3.3} $$

This formula displays the logical chain of Poisson dynamics:

$$ f\ \longmapsto\ \mathrm{d}f\ \longmapsto\ \pi^\sharp(\mathrm{d}f) = X_f\ \longmapsto\ \text{flow of }X_f. $$

The smooth function supplies a covector field, and the Poisson tensor converts those covectors into tangent directions.

3.1 The local coordinate formula#

Let $(x^1, \ldots, x^m)$ be local coordinates on an open set $U \subseteq M$. Every bivector field on $U$ can be written as

$$ \pi = \frac12\sum_{i, j = 1}^m\pi^{ij}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i} \wedge \frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}, \qquad \pi^{ij} = -\pi^{ji}. \tag{3.4} $$

The factor $1/2$ compensates for summing over both $(i, j)$ and $(j, i)$. Substituting (3.4) into (3.1) gives

$$ \lbrace f, g \rbrace = \sum_{i, j = 1}^m\pi^{ij}(x)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x^j}. \tag{3.5} $$

In particular,

$$ \pi^{ij} = \lbrace x^i, x^j \rbrace. $$

The Hamiltonian vector field of $f$ is therefore

$$ X_f = \sum_{i, j = 1}^m\pi^{ij}(x)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}. \tag{3.6} $$

Equations (3.4)–(3.6) are coordinate expressions of intrinsic objects. The coefficient functions $\pi^{ij}$ change under a change of coordinates, but the bivector $\pi$, the bracket, and the vector field $X_f$ do not depend on the chosen chart.

4. The Jacobi identity in coordinates#

Not every bivector field defines a Poisson bracket. Any bivector defines an antisymmetric biderivation through (3.1), but its bracket may fail the Jacobi identity. We now translate the missing condition into a differential equation for the coefficients $\pi^{ij}$.

Because the Jacobiator

$$ \operatorname{Jac}(f, g, h) = \lbrace f, \lbrace g, h \rbrace \rbrace + \lbrace g, \lbrace h, f \rbrace \rbrace + \lbrace h, \lbrace f, g \rbrace \rbrace $$

is a derivation in each argument, it is represented by a trivector field. It is therefore enough to evaluate it on coordinate functions. Since $\lbrace x^j, x^k \rbrace = \pi^{jk}$, we obtain

$$ \lbrace x^i, \lbrace x^j, x^k \rbrace \rbrace = \lbrace x^i, \pi^{jk} \rbrace = \sum_{\ell = 1}^m\pi^{i\ell}\frac{\partial\pi^{jk}}{\partial x^\ell}. $$

Adding the cyclic permutations gives the coordinate Jacobi condition:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{\ell = 1}^m \left( \pi^{i\ell}\frac{\partial\pi^{jk}}{\partial x^\ell} + \pi^{j\ell}\frac{\partial\pi^{ki}}{\partial x^\ell} + \pi^{k\ell}\frac{\partial\pi^{ij}}{\partial x^\ell} \right) = 0 } \tag{4.1} $$

for every $i, j, k \in \lbrace 1, \ldots, m \rbrace$.

This nonlinear first-order system is the coordinate test for a Poisson bivector. It immediately yields two useful facts.

First, every constant bivector is Poisson because all derivatives in (4.1) vanish. Canonical phase space is an example.

Second, every bivector on a two-dimensional manifold is Poisson. The Jacobiator is a trivector, but $\wedge^3 T_pM = \lbrace 0 \rbrace$ when $\dim M = 2$. Thus, in local coordinates $(x, y)$, every bivector

$$ \pi = \varphi(x, y)\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \wedge \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \qquad \varphi \in C^\infty(M), \tag{4.2} $$

defines a Poisson structure.

The corresponding statement is false in dimension three. For example, on $\mathbb{R}^3$ consider the bivector determined by

$$ \lbrace x, y \rbrace = x, \qquad \lbrace y, z \rbrace = y, \qquad \lbrace z, x \rbrace = z. $$

Its Jacobiator on the coordinate functions is

$$ \lbrace x, \lbrace y, z \rbrace \rbrace + \lbrace y, \lbrace z, x \rbrace \rbrace + \lbrace z, \lbrace x, y \rbrace \rbrace = x + y + z, $$

which is not identically zero. This is an almost-Poisson structure, not a Poisson structure.

4.1 The intrinsic Jacobi condition#

There is also a coordinate-free test. The Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket extends the Lie bracket of vector fields to multivector fields. For a bivector $\pi$, the expression $[\pi, \pi]_{\mathrm{SN}}$ is a trivector, and its coefficients are exactly the left-hand side of (4.1), up to the conventional normalization. Hence

$$ \boxed{\pi\text{ is Poisson}\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad [\pi, \pi]_{\mathrm{SN}} = 0.} \tag{4.3} $$

Equation (4.3) is the intrinsic tensor form of the Jacobi identity. It is especially valuable in deformation theory and Poisson cohomology, but the coordinate condition (4.1) is usually the more concrete entry point. Both descriptions are developed systematically by Crainic et al. (2021) and Bursztyn (2025, Sections 2.2–2.3).

5. Fundamental examples#

Examples reveal what the definition allows and why Poisson geometry is broader than symplectic geometry.

5.1 The zero Poisson structure#

Every smooth manifold admits the trivial bracket

$$ \lbrace f, g \rbrace = 0\qquad\text{for all }f, g \in C^\infty(M). $$

Equivalently, $\pi = 0$. Every Hamiltonian vector field vanishes, so every Hamiltonian trajectory is stationary. This example is degenerate in the strongest possible sense, but it is a legitimate Poisson manifold.

This observation is conceptually important: being a Poisson manifold imposes no restriction on the dimension or topology of the underlying smooth manifold. The nontrivial information lies in the chosen tensor $\pi$.

5.2 Symplectic manifolds#

A symplectic manifold is a pair $(M, \omega)$ in which $\omega \in \Omega^2(M)$ is closed and nondegenerate. Nondegeneracy means that

$$ \omega^\flat : TM \longrightarrow T^*M, \qquad \omega^\flat(X) = \iota_X\omega, $$

is a vector-bundle isomorphism. Therefore each $f \in C^\infty(M)$ determines a unique vector field $X_f$ through

$$ \iota_{X_f}\omega = \mathrm{d}f. \tag{5.1} $$

Define

$$ \lbrace f, g \rbrace = \mathrm{d}g(X_f) = \omega(X_g, X_f). \tag{5.2} $$

The closedness condition $\mathrm{d}\omega = 0$ implies that this bracket satisfies the Jacobi identity. Equivalently, the inverse bundle map

$$ \pi^\sharp = (\omega^\flat)^{-1} : T^*M \longrightarrow TM $$

defines a nondegenerate Poisson bivector. Conversely, every nondegenerate Poisson bivector arises from a unique symplectic form in this way. Thus

$$ \boxed{\text{symplectic structures}\quad \longleftrightarrow \quad\text{nondegenerate Poisson structures}.} \tag{5.3} $$

On canonical phase space, the symplectic form is

$$ \omega_{\mathrm{can}} = \sum_{i = 1}^n\mathrm{d}q^i \wedge \mathrm{d}p_i, $$

and its inverse Poisson bivector, in the convention of this article, is

$$ \pi_{\mathrm{can}} = \sum_{i = 1}^n\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i} \wedge \frac{\partial}{\partial q^i}. $$

Formula (3.1) then reproduces the canonical bracket (1.1).

5.3 Poisson structures on surfaces#

Let $M$ be a two-dimensional manifold. As observed after (4.2), every smooth bivector field on $M$ is Poisson. On $\mathbb{R}^2$, take

$$ \pi = \varphi(x, y)\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \wedge \frac{\partial}{\partial y}. $$

The bracket is

$$ \lbrace f, g \rbrace = \varphi(x, y) \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial g}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\right). \tag{5.4} $$

Where $\varphi \neq 0$, the Poisson structure is nondegenerate and therefore symplectic. Where $\varphi = 0$, its rank drops to zero. A single Poisson structure can consequently contain open two-dimensional regions of genuine motion and lower-dimensional sets on which every Hamiltonian vector field vanishes.

For example, if $\varphi(x, y) = x$, then the half-planes $x > 0$ and $x < 0$ are two-dimensional symplectic regions, while every point on the line $x = 0$ is a zero-dimensional symplectic leaf. This is the simplest picture of a singular Poisson manifold.

5.4 A Lie–Poisson structure on $\mathbb{R}^3$#

Let $\boldsymbol{\xi} = (x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Define

$$ \lbrace f, g \rbrace(\boldsymbol{\xi}) = \boldsymbol{\xi} \cdot \bigl(\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\xi}) \times \nabla g(\boldsymbol{\xi})\bigr). \tag{5.5} $$

Equivalently,

$$ \pi = z\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \wedge \frac{\partial}{\partial y} + x\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \wedge \frac{\partial}{\partial z} + y\frac{\partial}{\partial z} \wedge \frac{\partial}{\partial x}. \tag{5.6} $$

This is the linear Poisson structure on the dual of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$. Its coefficients are not constant, but they satisfy the Jacobi condition because the cross product encodes the Lie bracket of $\mathfrak{so}(3)$.

Consider the function

$$ C(x, y, z) = \frac12(x^2 + y^2 + z^2). $$

For every $g \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^3)$,

$$ \lbrace C, g \rbrace = \boldsymbol{\xi} \cdot \bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi} \times \nabla g\bigr) = 0. $$

Thus $X_C = 0$, even though $C$ is not constant on $\mathbb{R}^3$. Its level sets are spheres, and Hamiltonian dynamics generated by any $H$ remains on one of these spheres. The origin is exceptional: the bivector vanishes there.

For a rigid body with principal moments of inertia $I_1, I_2, I_3$, the Hamiltonian

$$ H(x, y, z) = \frac{x^2}{2I_1} + \frac{y^2}{2I_2} + \frac{z^2}{2I_3} $$

produces Euler’s rigid-body equations under this bracket. The motion is confined simultaneously to a level set of $H$ and a sphere $C = \text{constant}$. This example, discussed by Bursztyn (2025, Example 2.6), illustrates why degenerate Poisson structures are natural in mechanics: constraints on motion are encoded directly by the degeneracy of $\pi$.

6. Rank and the characteristic distribution#

The bundle map $\pi^\sharp : T^*M \to TM$ need not be invertible. At each $p \in M$, define the rank of $\pi$ at $p$ by

$$ \operatorname{rank}\pi\vert_p = \operatorname{rank}\bigl(\pi_p^\sharp : T_p^*M \to T_pM\bigr). $$

Because $\pi_p$ is skew-symmetric, this rank is always even. The image

$$ \mathcal{D}_p = \pi_p^\sharp(T_p^*M) \subseteq T_pM \tag{6.1} $$

is the space of all instantaneous directions generated by Hamiltonian functions at $p$. Indeed,

$$ \mathcal{D}_p = \left\lbrace X_f\vert_p \,\middle\vert\, f \in C^\infty(M) \right\rbrace. $$

The union

$$ \mathcal{D} = \pi^\sharp(T^*M) \subseteq TM $$

is called the characteristic distribution of the Poisson structure.

If the rank of $\pi$ is constant, $\mathcal{D}$ is a vector subbundle of $TM$, and the Poisson manifold is called regular. If the rank varies, $\mathcal{D}$ is a singular distribution: its fibers still form vector subspaces, but their dimensions need not be locally constant.

The Jacobi identity enters once again. By (2.2), commutators of Hamiltonian vector fields are Hamiltonian, so the family of directions in $\mathcal{D}$ is closed under Lie brackets. In the regular case, the Frobenius theorem then implies that $\mathcal{D}$ is tangent to a foliation. In the singular case, integrability is more subtle, but it still holds for Poisson structures; this follows locally from Weinstein’s splitting theorem.

7. Symplectic leaves#

Definition (symplectic leaf). A symplectic leaf of a Poisson manifold $(M, \pi)$ is a maximal connected immersed submanifold $\iota : \mathcal{O}\hookrightarrow M$ satisfying

$$ T_p\mathcal{O} = \mathcal{D}_p = \pi_p^\sharp(T_p^*M) $$

for every $p \in \mathcal{O}$.

This definition and the resulting symplectic foliation are developed in Bursztyn (2025, Section 4.1); their singular local structure is governed by Weinstein’s theorem below.

Every point of $M$ lies on exactly one symplectic leaf. Since $\mathcal{D}_{p}$ has even dimension, every leaf is even-dimensional. The tangent bivector induced by $\pi$ on a leaf is nondegenerate, and its inverse defines a symplectic form on that leaf.

More concretely, if $f, g \in C^\infty(M)$ and $p \in \mathcal{O}$, the leafwise symplectic form is characterized by

$$ \omega_{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(X_g\vert_p, X_f\vert_p\bigr) = \lbrace f, g \rbrace(p). \tag{7.1} $$

This is well defined even if several functions generate the same tangent vector: if $X_f\vert_p = 0$, then $\lbrace f, g \rbrace(p) = \mathrm{d}g\vert_p(X_f\vert_p) = 0$ for every $g$. The Jacobi identity implies that $\mathrm{d}\omega_{\mathcal{O}} = 0$, so $\omega_{\mathcal{O}}$ is genuinely symplectic. Conversely, a smooth symplectic foliation determines the Poisson tensor by inverting the symplectic form along each leaf; the smoothness requirement is essential.

The phrase “foliated by symplectic phase spaces” can now be understood literally:

  • a Hamiltonian vector field is tangent to every symplectic leaf;
  • a Hamiltonian trajectory can never jump from one leaf to another;
  • within each leaf, the dynamics is ordinary symplectic Hamiltonian dynamics;
  • different leaves may have different dimensions when the Poisson rank is not constant.

For the zero Poisson structure, every leaf is a point. For a symplectic manifold, the connected components of $M$ are the leaves. For the surface structure $\pi = x, \partial_x \wedge \partial_y$, the two open half-planes are two-dimensional leaves and each point of the line $x = 0$ is a zero-dimensional leaf. For the Lie–Poisson structure (5.6), the nonzero leaves are the spheres centered at the origin, while the origin itself is a point leaf.

One subtlety is worth emphasizing: leaves are generally immersed, not necessarily embedded. A leaf can wind densely through part of the ambient manifold. The word “foliation” must therefore be interpreted in the regular or singular geometric sense, not as a guarantee that the leaf space $M/\mathcal{O}$ is a well-behaved manifold.

8. Casimir functions#

Definition (Casimir). A function $C \in C^\infty(M)$ is a Casimir function if

$$ \lbrace C, f \rbrace = 0\qquad\text{for every }f \in C^\infty(M). \tag{8.1} $$

Equivalently,

$$ X_C = 0\qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \pi^\sharp(\mathrm{d}C) = 0. $$

By skew-symmetry, (8.1) also gives $X_f(C) = \lbrace f, C \rbrace = 0$ for every Hamiltonian $f$. Hence a Casimir is constant along every Hamiltonian trajectory and, more strongly, constant on every connected symplectic leaf.

Casimirs and ordinary first integrals play different roles:

  • A first integral of a particular Hamiltonian $H$ satisfies $\lbrace H, f \rbrace = 0$ and depends on the chosen dynamics.
  • A Casimir satisfies $\lbrace g, C \rbrace = 0$ for every $g$ and is determined by the Poisson structure itself.

On a symplectic manifold, $\pi^\sharp$ is invertible, so $\pi^\sharp(\mathrm{d}C) = 0$ implies $\mathrm{d}C = 0$. Therefore Casimirs are locally constant, and they are constant when the manifold is connected. Nonconstant Casimirs are a signature of degeneracy.

In favorable regular regions, sufficiently many independent Casimirs locally describe the symplectic leaves as their common level sets. If $M$ has dimension $m$, $\pi$ has constant rank $2r$, and one can find $m - 2r$ Casimirs with linearly independent differentials, then connected components of their common level sets are the symplectic leaves.

This is not a global theorem without additional hypotheses. A Poisson manifold may have too few global Casimir functions to separate its leaves, and its leaf space may be singular or non-Hausdorff. It is therefore safer to regard Casimirs as useful detectors of leaves, not as a universal global coordinate system for the leaf space.

9. Poisson maps#

The natural maps in Poisson geometry are those that preserve brackets.

Definition (Poisson map). Let $(M_1, \lbrace \cdot, \cdot \rbrace_1)$ and $(M_2, \lbrace \cdot, \cdot \rbrace_2)$ be Poisson manifolds. A smooth map $\Phi : M_1 \to M_2$ is a Poisson map if

$$ \lbrace f, g \rbrace_2 \circ \Phi = \lbrace f \circ \Phi, g \circ \Phi \rbrace_1 \tag{9.1} $$

for all $f, g \in C^\infty(M_2)$.

Equation (9.1) says that pulling functions back by $\Phi$ preserves the Poisson algebra. It also implies compatibility of Hamiltonian vector fields: the Hamiltonian vector field of $f \circ \Phi$ on $M_1$ is $\Phi$-related to the Hamiltonian vector field of $f$ on $M_2$.

In tensorial language, the same condition is

$$ \pi_2^\sharp\vert_{\Phi(p)} = \mathrm{d}_p\Phi \circ \pi_1^\sharp\vert_p \circ (\mathrm{d}_p\Phi)^*, \tag{9.2} $$

where $(\mathrm{d}p\Phi)^* : T{\Phi(p)}^*M_2 \to T_p^*M_1$ is the cotangent pullback.

If $\Phi$ is a Poisson diffeomorphism, it maps symplectic leaves of $M_1$ to symplectic leaves of $M_2$ and restricts to a symplectomorphism on each leaf. Thus Poisson diffeomorphisms preserve both the singular decomposition into leaves and the symplectic geometry carried by those leaves.

For a diffeomorphism between symplectic manifolds, preserving the symplectic forms is equivalent to being a Poisson map. The two conditions are not equivalent for a general, noninvertible smooth map: a form-preserving map must be an immersion, whereas a Poisson map may instead be a submersion. Invertibility is what makes the covariant condition on forms and the contravariant condition on bivectors equivalent.

10. Weinstein’s local splitting theorem#

The central local structure theorem for Poisson manifolds was proved by Weinstein (1983). It explains why symplectic leaves are the correct local building blocks.

Theorem (Weinstein splitting theorem). Let $(M, \pi)$ be an $m$-dimensional Poisson manifold, and suppose that $\pi$ has rank $2r$ at $p \in M$. Then there are local coordinates $(q^1, \ldots, q^r, p_1, \ldots, p_r, y^1, \ldots, y^s)$ centered at $p$, where $s = m - 2r$, in which $\pi$ takes the following form:

$$ \pi = \sum_{i = 1}^r\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i} \wedge \frac{\partial}{\partial q^i} + \frac12\sum_{a, b = 1}^s\varphi^{ab}(\mathbf y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^a} \wedge \frac{\partial}{\partial y^b}, \qquad \varphi^{ab}(\mathbf 0) = 0. \tag{10.1} $$

Here each coefficient $\varphi^{ab}$ depends only on the transverse coordinates $\mathbf y = (y^1, \ldots, y^s)$. The first term in (10.1) is a canonical symplectic Poisson structure. The second is a transverse Poisson structure that vanishes at the chosen point. Locally, therefore, a Poisson manifold splits into

$$ \text{a symplectic factor}\quad \times \quad\text{a transverse Poisson factor vanishing at the base point}. $$

Several important facts follow.

  1. The Poisson rank at a point is even.
  2. Through every point passes a symplectic leaf of dimension equal to that rank.
  3. If the rank is locally constant, the transverse term vanishes throughout a sufficiently small neighborhood, so the Poisson tensor has the canonical form $\sum_i\partial_{p_i} \wedge \partial_{q^i}$ there.
  4. If $\pi$ is nondegenerate, there is no transverse factor, and the theorem reduces to Darboux’s theorem for symplectic manifolds.

The theorem does not say that every singular Poisson structure is locally linear, nor does it remove all transverse complexity. It isolates that complexity in the $\mathbf y$ variables. Understanding when the transverse structure can be simplified further is a deeper problem in Poisson geometry.

11. What a Poisson structure does—and does not—provide#

It is useful to separate the data supplied by a Poisson structure from geometric structures that must be chosen independently.

In particular, a Poisson structure is not a metric. It does not measure lengths, angles, or distances. It is also not itself a dynamical system: it provides the mechanism $H \mapsto X_H$, but one must still choose the Hamiltonian.

Nor is a Poisson structure merely an antisymmetric matrix in coordinates. A coordinate matrix represents a bivector field, and its entries must transform tensorially between charts. If the entries vary with position, they must also satisfy the nonlinear Jacobi condition (4.1).

12. Common misconceptions#

12.1 “Every Poisson manifold is symplectic”#

False. A Poisson manifold is symplectic only when $\pi^\sharp : T^*M \to TM$ is invertible everywhere. The zero bracket and the Lie–Poisson structure on $\mathbb{R}^3$ are Poisson but not symplectic.

12.2 “A Poisson manifold must have even dimension”#

False. Its symplectic leaves have even dimensions, but the ambient manifold can have any dimension. The three-dimensional Lie–Poisson example is the standard counterexample.

12.3 “Skew-symmetry and the Leibniz rule are sufficient”#

False in dimensions three and higher. They define an almost-Poisson bracket. The Jacobi identity is an independent differential constraint, as the nonexample following (4.2) demonstrates.

12.4 “A Casimir has zero differential”#

Not necessarily. A Casimir satisfies $\pi^\sharp(\mathrm{d}C) = 0$. Its differential may be nonzero but lie in the kernel of $\pi^\sharp$. Only in the symplectic case does invertibility force $\mathrm{d}C = 0$.

12.5 “Symplectic leaves are always embedded level surfaces”#

False. Locally, regular leaves often look like common level sets of Casimirs, and many familiar examples are embedded. Globally, leaves may be immersed but not embedded, and there may not be enough global Casimirs to describe them.

12.6 “The Jacobi identity is only an algebraic convention”#

False. It ensures the closure relation $[X_f, X_g] = X_{\lbrace f, g \rbrace}$, the invariance of the Poisson tensor under Hamiltonian flows, and the integrability that ultimately leads to symplectic leaves.

13. Final perspective#

The definition of a Poisson manifold can be read in three mutually reinforcing ways.

The algebraic viewpoint begins with observables. The space $C^\infty(M)$ is both a commutative algebra and a Lie algebra, and the Leibniz rule connects these two structures.

The tensorial viewpoint packages the bracket into a bivector $\pi$. The Jacobi identity becomes the geometric differential equation $[\pi, \pi]_{\mathrm{SN}} = 0$, while $\pi^\sharp$ converts differentials into Hamiltonian vector fields.

The foliated viewpoint follows the image of $\pi^\sharp$. Its integral manifolds are symplectic leaves, and every Hamiltonian trajectory remains inside one leaf. Degeneracy is not a defect: it records which directions are dynamically accessible and which quantities, such as Casimirs, constrain all Hamiltonian motion.

These viewpoints fit into one chain:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Poisson bracket} \ \longleftrightarrow \ \text{Poisson bivector} \ \longrightarrow \ \text{Hamiltonian vector fields} \ \longrightarrow \ \text{symplectic leaves} } $$

The essential message is that Poisson geometry extends Hamiltonian mechanics beyond a single nondegenerate phase space. It allows phase spaces with constraints, singularities, symmetries, and variable rank while retaining a coherent notion of Hamiltonian evolution. That is why Poisson manifolds appear naturally wherever geometry, dynamics, and algebra meet.
