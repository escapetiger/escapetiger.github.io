Abstract. The classical Laplacian asks for two derivatives, but weak solutions of elliptic and parabolic equations often possess only one square-integrable derivative. This post develops the canonical Gelfand triple $H_0^1(\Omega) \hookrightarrow L^2(\Omega) \hookrightarrow H^{-1}(\Omega)$ from first principles. We explain what each embedding means, construct the weak Dirichlet Laplacian as a bounded map from $H_0^1(\Omega)$ to $H^{-1}(\Omega)$, distinguish it from the unbounded Laplacian on $L^2(\Omega)$, and show why this framework is natural for the Poisson and heat equations.

The classical formula

$$ \Delta u = \sum_{i = 1}^{d} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x_i^2} $$

appears to require two derivatives of $u$. That is too restrictive for many partial differential equations. A weak solution of the Dirichlet problem may have a square-integrable gradient while its second derivatives exist only as distributions. For such a solution, asking whether $\Delta u$ belongs to $L^2(\Omega)$ may be the wrong question.

The more useful question is this:

Guiding question. If $u$ has one weak derivative in $L^2(\Omega)$ and vanishes on the boundary, in which space does its Laplacian naturally live?

The answer is $H^{-1}(\Omega)$, the dual of $H_0^1(\Omega)$. This leads to the chain

$$ \boxed{H_0^1(\Omega) \hookrightarrow L^2(\Omega) \hookrightarrow H^{-1}(\Omega)}. $$

This chain is not merely a convenient list of spaces. It records how spatial regularity, energy, and duality interact in weak formulations.

1. Setting and conventions#

Throughout the post, let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be a nonempty bounded open set with Lipschitz boundary. Boundedness gives the Poincaré inequality on $H_0^1(\Omega)$, while the Lipschitz assumption allows us to interpret membership in $H_0^1(\Omega)$ as a zero trace condition.

All function spaces are real. The complex-valued theory is analogous, with the usual conjugation in inner products and duality pairings.

For scalar functions $u$ and $v$, write

$$ (u, v)_{L^2} = \int_{\Omega} u(x)v(x)\,\mathrm{d}x, \qquad \lVert u \rVert_{L^2}^2 = (u, u)_{L^2}. $$

The notation $\langle F, v \rangle_{H^{-1}, H_0^1}$ denotes the action of a functional $F \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$ on a test function $v \in H_0^1(\Omega)$. When the spaces are clear, we shorten it to $\langle F, v \rangle$.

The standard variational theory used below may be found, for example, in Brezis (2011, Chapters 9–11). The time-dependent Gelfand-triple framework is developed systematically by Lions and Magenes (1972, Chapter III).

2. From classical derivatives to Sobolev spaces#

2.1 Weak derivatives#

Let $u \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$. A function $g_i \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$ is the weak derivative $\partial_i u$ if

$$ \int_{\Omega} g_i \varphi\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_{\Omega} u\,\partial_i \varphi\,\mathrm{d}x $$

for every $\varphi \in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$. This identity is obtained from classical integration by parts, but here it is used as the definition. No boundary term appears because $\varphi$ has compact support in $\Omega$.

Definition ($H^1$). The Sobolev space $H^1(\Omega)$ consists of all $u \in L^2(\Omega)$ whose first weak derivatives $\partial_i u$ belong to $L^2(\Omega)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, d$.

Its norm is

$$ \lVert u \rVert_{H^1}^2 = \lVert u \rVert_{L^2}^2 + \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}^2, \qquad \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}^2 = \sum_{i = 1}^{d} \lVert \partial_i u \rVert_{L^2}^2. $$

Equipped with the inner product associated with this norm, $H^1(\Omega)$ is a Hilbert space.

2.2 The zero-boundary space $H_0^1(\Omega)$#

Definition ($H_0^1$). The space $H_0^1(\Omega)$ is the closure of $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ in the $H^1(\Omega)$ norm.

Thus $u \in H_0^1(\Omega)$ precisely when there exists a sequence $(u_n)_{n \ge 1} \subset C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ such that

$$ u_n \to u \quad \text{in } H^1(\Omega). $$

The closure definition is valid for every open set. Under our Lipschitz-boundary assumption, the trace operator is well defined and

$$ H_0^1(\Omega) = \left\lbrace u \in H^1(\Omega) : \operatorname{Tr}u = 0 \text{ on } \partial\Omega \right\rbrace. $$

One should read the boundary condition in the trace sense. A general Sobolev function need not possess pointwise boundary values.

The Poincaré inequality, in the Sobolev-space form presented by Brezis (2011, Chapters 9–10), states that there is a constant $C_P > 0$, depending on $\Omega$, such that

$$ \lVert u \rVert_{L^2} \le C_P \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2} $$

for every $u \in H_0^1(\Omega)$. Consequently,

$$ \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2} \le \lVert u \rVert_{H^1} \le \sqrt{1 + C_P^2}\,\lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}. $$

The gradient seminorm is therefore a genuine norm on $H_0^1(\Omega)$ and is equivalent to the full $H^1$ norm. From now on, we use

$$ \lVert u \rVert_{H_0^1} := \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}, \qquad (u, v)_{H_0^1} := \int_{\Omega} \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x. $$

This choice will make the relation between $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ and the Dirichlet Laplacian exact rather than merely equivalent up to constants.

3. The dual space $H^{-1}(\Omega)$#

Definition ($H^{-1}$). The space $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ is the continuous dual of $H_0^1(\Omega)$:

$$ H^{-1}(\Omega) := \bigl(H_0^1(\Omega)\bigr)'. $$

Its norm is

$$ \lVert F \rVert_{H^{-1}} = \sup_{\substack{v \in H_0^1(\Omega) \\ v \ne 0}} \frac{\lvert \langle F, v \rangle \rvert}{\lVert \nabla v \rVert_{L^2}}. $$

An element of $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ need not be an ordinary function. It is a continuous linear rule that accepts one $H_0^1$ test function and returns a number.

3.1 Every $L^2$ function defines an $H^{-1}$ functional#

Given $f \in L^2(\Omega)$, define $Jf$ by

$$ \langle Jf, v \rangle := \int_{\Omega} f v\,\mathrm{d}x $$

for $v \in H_0^1(\Omega)$. The Cauchy–Schwarz and Poincaré inequalities give

$$ \begin{aligned} \lvert \langle Jf, v \rangle \rvert &\le \lVert f \rVert_{L^2}\lVert v \rVert_{L^2} \\ &\le C_P \lVert f \rVert_{L^2}\lVert \nabla v \rVert_{L^2}. \end{aligned} $$

Therefore $Jf \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$ and

$$ \lVert Jf \rVert_{H^{-1}} \le C_P \lVert f \rVert_{L^2}. $$

The map $J : L^2(\Omega) \to H^{-1}(\Omega)$ is linear, continuous, and injective. We normally identify $f$ with $Jf$ and write

$$ L^2(\Omega) \hookrightarrow H^{-1}(\Omega). $$

This is an identification through the $L^2$ pairing, not a literal claim that a function is already a functional without specifying how it acts.

3.2 Divergences of square-integrable vector fields#

The space $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ also contains distributions that need not belong to $L^2(\Omega)$. Let $G \in L^2(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$ and define its distributional divergence by

$$ \langle \mathrm{div}\,G, v \rangle := -\int_{\Omega} G \cdot \nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x. $$

Then

$$ \lvert \langle \mathrm{div}\,G, v \rangle \rvert \le \lVert G \rVert_{L^2}\lVert \nabla v \rVert_{L^2}, $$

so

$$ \mathrm{div}\,G \in H^{-1}(\Omega), \qquad \lVert \mathrm{div}\,G \rVert_{H^{-1}} \le \lVert G \rVert_{L^2}. $$

This observation already suggests why the Laplacian of an $H_0^1$ function belongs to $H^{-1}$: if $u \in H_0^1(\Omega)$, then $\nabla u \in L^2(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$ and $\Delta u = \mathrm{div}(\nabla u)$ in the distributional sense.

4. Constructing the Gelfand triple#

Let

$$ V = H_0^1(\Omega), \qquad H = L^2(\Omega), \qquad V' = H^{-1}(\Omega). $$

The first embedding $V \hookrightarrow H$ is continuous by Poincaré’s inequality. It is dense because $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ is contained in $H_0^1(\Omega)$ and is dense in $L^2(\Omega)$.

The Hilbert space $H$ is identified with its dual $H’$ by the Riesz map

$$ f \mapsto (f, \cdot)_{L^2}. $$

Restricting this functional from $H$ to $V$ gives precisely the map $J : H \to V’$ constructed above. Hence

$$ V \hookrightarrow H \cong H' \hookrightarrow V'. $$

Definition (Gelfand triple). A Gelfand triple, also called an evolution triple, is a chain $V \hookrightarrow H \cong H’ \hookrightarrow V’$ in which $V$ is a Hilbert or reflexive Banach space, $H$ is a Hilbert space identified with its dual, and the embedding $V \hookrightarrow H$ is continuous and dense. This is the functional setting used for variational evolution equations by Lions and Magenes (1972, Chapter III).

In the present setting, we obtain the canonical triple

$$ \boxed{H_0^1(\Omega) \hookrightarrow L^2(\Omega) \hookrightarrow H^{-1}(\Omega)}. $$

Both embeddings are continuous and dense. Density of $L^2(\Omega)$ in $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ follows abstractly because the adjoint of the injective map $H_0^1(\Omega) \hookrightarrow L^2(\Omega)$ has dense range. Equivalently, no nonzero element of $H_0^1(\Omega)$ can annihilate every $L^2$ functional.

The three spaces measure different levels of regularity:

  • $H_0^1(\Omega)$ contains functions with one square-integrable weak derivative and zero trace;
  • $L^2(\Omega)$ measures square-integrable size but does not require a derivative;
  • $H^{-1}(\Omega)$ contains continuous functionals on $H_0^1(\Omega)$ and may include genuine distributions.

A one-dimensional scale test#

Take $\Omega = (0, \pi)$ and the normalized Dirichlet eigenfunctions

$$ e_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sin(nx), \qquad n \ge 1. $$

They satisfy $-e_n’’ = n^2 e_n$. With the norms chosen above,

$$ \lVert e_n \rVert_{H_0^1} = n, \qquad \lVert e_n \rVert_{L^2} = 1, \qquad \lVert e_n \rVert_{H^{-1}} = \frac{1}{n}. $$

Thus high-frequency oscillations are large in $H_0^1$, neutral in $L^2$, and small in $H^{-1}$. This is the regularity hierarchy encoded by the triple.

The inclusions are genuinely strict. On $(0, \pi)$, the constant function $1$ belongs to $L^2$ but not to $H_0^1$. Moreover, point evaluation is continuous on $H_0^1(0, \pi)$, so the Dirac mass $\delta_a$ at any $a \in (0, \pi)$ belongs to $H^{-1}(0, \pi)$ but not to $L^2(0, \pi)$.

5. The weak Dirichlet Laplacian#

For a smooth function $u$ that vanishes on the boundary, integration by parts gives

$$ \int_{\Omega} (-\Delta u)v\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\Omega} \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x. $$

The right-hand side still makes sense when $u$ and $v$ belong only to $H_0^1(\Omega)$. This motivates the following definition.

Definition (weak Dirichlet Laplacian). Define $A : H_0^1(\Omega) \to H^{-1}(\Omega)$ by

$$ \langle Au, v \rangle := \int_{\Omega} \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x $$

for every $u, v \in H_0^1(\Omega)$. In distributional notation, $Au = -\Delta u$.

The sign is worth emphasizing. The positive operator associated with the Dirichlet energy is $A = -\Delta$, because

$$ \langle Au, u \rangle = \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}^2 \ge 0. $$

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

$$ \lvert \langle Au, v \rangle \rvert \le \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}\lVert \nabla v \rVert_{L^2}, $$

and therefore

$$ \lVert Au \rVert_{H^{-1}} \le \lVert u \rVert_{H_0^1}. $$

In fact equality holds. If $u \ne 0$, choose $v = u / \lVert u \rVert_{H_0^1}$ in the dual norm. Then

$$ \lVert Au \rVert_{H^{-1}} = \lVert u \rVert_{H_0^1}. $$

By the Riesz representation theorem, $A$ is onto $H^{-1}(\Omega)$. Thus $A$ is the Riesz isometric isomorphism from $H_0^1(\Omega)$ onto its dual when $H_0^1(\Omega)$ is equipped with the gradient inner product.

6. The Poisson problem and the exact negative norm#

Let $F \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$. The weak homogeneous Dirichlet problem

$$ -\Delta u = F \quad \text{in } \Omega, \qquad u = 0 \quad \text{on } \partial\Omega $$

means:

Weak formulation. Find $u \in H_0^1(\Omega)$ such that

$$ \int_{\Omega} \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x = \langle F, v \rangle $$

for every $v \in H_0^1(\Omega)$.

The bilinear form

$$ a(u, v) = \int_{\Omega} \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x $$

is continuous and coercive on $H_0^1(\Omega)$. The Lax–Milgram theorem therefore gives a unique weak solution $u \in H_0^1(\Omega)$ for every $F \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$; see Brezis (2011, Chapter 5) for the Hilbert-space theorem. In operator notation,

$$ u = A^{-1}F = (-\Delta)^{-1}F. $$

Since $A$ is an isometry for our norm convention,

$$ \boxed{\lVert F \rVert_{H^{-1}} = \left\lVert \nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}F \right\rVert_{L^2}}. $$

Testing the weak equation with $v = u$ also gives

$$ \lVert F \rVert_{H^{-1}}^2 = \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}^2 = \langle F, u \rangle. $$

These are exact identities because we defined the $H_0^1$ norm by $\lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}$. If the full $H^1$ norm is used instead, the corresponding dual norm is equivalent to this one, but the displayed equality must be replaced by two-sided estimates.

The weak solution is also the unique minimizer of the energy

$$ \mathcal{E}(v) = \frac{1}{2}\lVert \nabla v \rVert_{L^2}^2 - \langle F, v \rangle $$

over $H_0^1(\Omega)$. Indeed, the first variation in a direction $w \in H_0^1(\Omega)$ is

$$ \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mathcal{E}(u + \varepsilon w)\right\vert_{\varepsilon = 0} = \int_{\Omega} \nabla u \cdot \nabla w\,\mathrm{d}x - \langle F, w \rangle, $$

which vanishes for every $w$ exactly when $u$ satisfies the weak Poisson equation.

The quadratic term is strictly convex and coercive on $H_0^1(\Omega)$, so this critical point is the unique global minimizer.

Example: a point source in one dimension#

Let $\Omega = (0, 1)$ and $F = \delta_a$ for some $a \in (0, 1)$. Since $H_0^1(0, 1)$ embeds continuously into $C([0, 1])$, the map $v \mapsto v(a)$ is continuous on $H_0^1(0, 1)$; hence $\delta_a \in H^{-1}(0, 1)$.

The weak solution of

$$ -u'' = \delta_a, \qquad u(0) = u(1) = 0, $$

is the piecewise linear function

$$ u(x) = \begin{cases} x(1 - a), & 0 \le x \le a, \\ a(1 - x), & a \le x \le 1. \end{cases} $$

Its derivative has a jump of size $-1$ at $x = a$, so $u’’ = -\delta_a$ in the distributional sense. The function belongs to $H_0^1(0, 1)$ but not to $H^2(0, 1)$. This is precisely the kind of solution that the weak Laplacian captures and the classical second-derivative viewpoint misses.

7. One symbol, three realizations of the Laplacian#

The notation $-\Delta$ is used for several related operators. Their domains and codomains must not be confused.

7.1 The classical realization#

If $u$ is twice continuously differentiable, then

$$ -\Delta u = -\sum_{i = 1}^{d} \partial_i^2 u $$

is an ordinary function defined pointwise.

7.2 The variational realization#

The weak operator

$$ A : H_0^1(\Omega) \to H^{-1}(\Omega) $$

is bounded and defined for every $u \in H_0^1(\Omega)$. This is the natural realization for energy solutions and variational formulations.

7.3 The unbounded realization on $L^2$#

The Dirichlet Laplacian on the pivot space $L^2(\Omega)$ is the operator

$$ A_2 : D(A_2) \subset L^2(\Omega) \to L^2(\Omega), $$

with domain

$$ D(A_2) = \left\lbrace u \in H_0^1(\Omega) : -\Delta u \in L^2(\Omega) \text{ in the distributional sense} \right\rbrace $$

and $A_2u = -\Delta u$. It is an unbounded, positive, self-adjoint operator on $L^2(\Omega)$. If the boundary is sufficiently regular, for example of class $C^{1,1}$, elliptic regularity identifies its domain as

$$ D(A_2) = H^2(\Omega) \cap H_0^1(\Omega). $$

This identification can fail on nonsmooth domains. The variational domain $H_0^1(\Omega)$ remains meaningful even when global $H^2$ regularity is unavailable; compare the elliptic variational and regularity theory in Brezis (2011, Chapters 9–10).

The distinction may be summarized as follows:

  • $A : H_0^1 \to H^{-1}$ is bounded and defined on the entire energy space;
  • $A_2 : D(A_2) \subset L^2 \to L^2$ is unbounded and has a smaller domain;
  • both represent the same distributional expression $-\Delta$, but in different functional settings.

8. Why the triple is natural for the heat equation#

Consider the homogeneous Dirichlet heat equation with $F \in L^2(0, T; H^{-1}(\Omega))$:

$$ \partial_t u - \Delta u = F. $$

If $u(t) \in H_0^1(\Omega)$, then $-\Delta u(t) \in H^{-1}(\Omega)$. It is therefore natural to seek solutions satisfying

$$ u \in L^2(0, T; H_0^1(\Omega)), \qquad \partial_t u \in L^2(0, T; H^{-1}(\Omega)). $$

The equation is interpreted as

$$ \langle \partial_t u(t), v \rangle

  • \int_{\Omega} \nabla u(t) \cdot \nabla v,\mathrm{d}x = \langle F(t), v \rangle $$

for every $v \in H_0^1(\Omega)$ and almost every $t \in (0, T)$.

The fundamental Lions–Magenes result for a Gelfand triple states that

$$ u \in L^2(0, T; V), \qquad \partial_t u \in L^2(0, T; V') $$

imply that $u$ has a representative in $C([0, T]; H)$ and

$$ \frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert u(t) \rVert_H^2 = \langle \partial_t u(t), u(t) \rangle_{V', V} $$

for almost every $t \in (0, T)$; see Lions and Magenes (1972, Chapter III). In our canonical triple, this becomes

$$ \frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert u(t) \rVert_{L^2}^2 = \langle \partial_t u(t), u(t) \rangle_{H^{-1}, H_0^1}. $$

We may therefore test the weak heat equation with $v = u(t)$ and obtain the energy identity

$$ \frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert u(t) \rVert_{L^2}^2

  • \lVert \nabla u(t) \rVert_{L^2}^2 = \langle F(t), u(t) \rangle. $$

This identity reveals the division of labor among the three spaces:

  • $H_0^1$ controls spatial energy through $\nabla u$;
  • $L^2$ stores the evolving energy $\lVert u(t) \rVert_{L^2}^2 / 2$;
  • $H^{-1}$ is large enough to contain both the weak Laplacian and the time derivative.

That is why the Gelfand triple is more than formal notation: it is exactly the structure needed to make the weak equation and its energy law compatible.

9. Common points of confusion#

The middle inclusion is not automatic set containment#

An $L^2$ function enters $H^{-1}$ through the map

$$ f \mapsto \left(v \mapsto \int_{\Omega} fv\,\mathrm{d}x\right). $$

The pairing is part of the identification.

Elements of $H^{-1}$ need not be functions#

They are continuous linear functionals on $H_0^1$. Depending on the dimension, they may include measures or derivatives of $L^2$ functions. A Dirac mass belongs to $H^{-1}$ in one space dimension, but not in dimensions $d \ge 2$.

The Laplacian does not generally map $H_0^1$ into $L^2$#

What always holds is

$$ \Delta : H_0^1(\Omega) \to H^{-1}(\Omega). $$

Membership of $\Delta u$ in $L^2$ is additional regularity and defines the smaller domain $D(A_2)$.

The positive operator is $-\Delta$#

With homogeneous Dirichlet conditions,

$$ \langle -\Delta u, u \rangle = \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}^2. $$

Using $\Delta$ instead reverses the sign of the quadratic form.

The exact $H^{-1}$ formula depends on the chosen equivalent norm#

The identity

$$ \lVert F \rVert_{H^{-1}} = \left\lVert \nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}F \right\rVert_{L^2} $$

uses $\lVert u \rVert_{H_0^1} = \lVert \nabla u \rVert_{L^2}$. With the full $H^1$ norm, one obtains an equivalent dual norm, not the same numerical value.

10. Final perspective#

The canonical Gelfand triple is built from three simple facts:

  1. A zero-trace $H^1$ function is square integrable, so $H_0^1(\Omega) \hookrightarrow L^2(\Omega)$.
  2. An $L^2$ function acts continuously on $H_0^1$ by integration, so $L^2(\Omega) \hookrightarrow H^{-1}(\Omega)$.
  3. The gradient of an $H_0^1$ function is in $L^2$, so its distributional divergence—and hence its Laplacian—belongs to $H^{-1}$.

The result is the exact setting in which the Dirichlet Laplacian becomes a bounded isomorphism

$$ -\Delta : H_0^1(\Omega) \to H^{-1}(\Omega), $$

while $L^2(\Omega)$ remains the pivot space for energy and time evolution. Once this architecture is clear, weak formulations of the Poisson and heat equations no longer look like formal replacements for classical derivatives. They become the natural equations dictated by the regularity of the solutions.
