Abstract. A smooth manifold is a space that may be globally curved or topologically complicated while remaining locally indistinguishable from Euclidean space. This post introduces the subject from scratch, with a notation guide and a sequence of geometric illustrations. We begin with topology, charts, transition maps, and atlases; then define smooth functions and smooth maps without assuming that the manifold sits inside a larger Euclidean space. From there, we construct tangent and cotangent spaces, differentials, vector fields, integral curves, flows, and Lie brackets. The sphere serves as a recurring example, allowing each abstract definition to be compared with familiar geometry. The central theme is that coordinates make calculations possible, while compatibility under coordinate changes makes the resulting mathematics intrinsic.

Imagine a tiny observer living on the surface of a sphere. The observer can draw a local map, measure two coordinates, differentiate functions, and follow curves without ever leaving the surface. What the observer cannot do is describe the entire sphere by one regular Cartesian coordinate system. Any single map must either omit a point, overlap itself, or become singular somewhere.

This is exactly the problem that manifold theory solves. A manifold is allowed to require many local maps, provided that the maps agree smoothly wherever they overlap. Calculus is performed inside the maps, while the compatibility of the maps guarantees that the answers describe the underlying space rather than a particular choice of coordinates.

The guiding principle is therefore

$$\boxed{\text{A smooth }d\text{-dimensional manifold is locally modeled on }\mathbb{R}^d.}$$

The word locally carries most of the meaning. A circle is locally a line but is not globally a line. A sphere is locally a plane but is not globally a plane. Manifold theory turns this local Euclidean behavior into a rigorous setting for calculus.

Convention. Unless stated otherwise, every manifold in this article is finite-dimensional, Hausdorff, second countable, smooth, and without boundary.

The terminology and conventions below follow the standard treatments of Lee (2012) and Tu (2011). The topological preliminaries are consistent with Lee (2011).

How to read the notation#

The same few types of symbols recur throughout the article. Fixing their meaning now makes the later formulas easier to read.

  • $M$, $N$, and $P$ denote manifolds. The letters $p$ and $q$ denote points on them.
  • $d$, $m$, $n$, and $k$ denote nonnegative integers used for dimensions or indices. For example, $\mathbb{R}^d$ is the set of ordered $d$-tuples of real numbers.
  • Superscripts such as $x^i$ label coordinate functions; they are not powers. Unless a different range is stated, $i$ and $j$ range from $1$ to $d$.
  • The expression $p \in M$ means that $p$ belongs to $M$, while $U \subseteq M$ means that every point of $U$ belongs to $M$. The expressions $U \cap V$, $U \cup V$, and $M \setminus U$ denote intersection, union, and set difference, respectively; $\varnothing$ denotes the empty set.
  • Set-builder notation such as $\left\lbrace x \in M : \mathcal{P}(x) \right\rbrace$ means “the set of points $x$ in $M$ for which the stated property $\mathcal{P}(x)$ holds.” The colon is read as “such that.”
  • $F : M \to N$ means that $F$ is a map with domain $M$ and codomain $N$. The symbol $\circ$ denotes composition, $F^{-1}$ denotes an inverse map when one exists, and $\operatorname{id}_M$ denotes the identity map $p \mapsto p$ on $M$. When $V \subseteq N$, the notation $F^{-1}(V) = \left\lbrace p \in M : F(p) \in V \right\rbrace$ means the preimage of $V$ and does not require $F$ to be invertible.
  • The expression $\sum_{i = 1}^d a_i$ means $a_1 + \cdots + a_d$. The letter $i$ is the summation index, while the lower and upper limits $1$ and $d$ specify its range.
  • $C^\infty(M)$ denotes the real-valued smooth functions on $M$. The superscript $\infty$ means differentiable of every finite order.
  • $D h_a$ denotes the ordinary total derivative, or Jacobian linear map, of a Euclidean map $h$ at a Euclidean point $a$. By contrast, $dF_p$ denotes the intrinsic differential of a smooth map $F$ at a manifold point $p$.
  • $T_pM$ and $T_p^\ast M$ denote the tangent and cotangent spaces at $p$. The symbol $\ast$ in $T_p^\ast M$ means algebraic dual; it does not denote multiplication.
  • $\bigsqcup$ denotes disjoint union. It is used for bundles because vectors attached to different base points must remain distinct even if their coordinate components happen to agree.
  • $\langle \alpha_p, v \rangle$ denotes the evaluation of a covector $\alpha_p$ on a tangent vector $v$. In embedded sphere examples only, $u \cdot v$ denotes the ordinary Euclidean inner product.

Further symbols are defined at the point where they first become useful. In particular, a subscript usually records a base point or a coordinate label, whereas a superscript usually records a coordinate component.

1. Why Euclidean calculus is not enough#

Classical multivariable calculus is formulated on open subsets

$$\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d.$$

The Greek letter $\Omega$ names an arbitrary open coordinate domain, and $x = (x^1, \ldots, x^d)$ denotes one of its points. The openness of $\Omega$ matters. If $x \in \Omega$, then sufficiently small changes of every coordinate remain inside $\Omega$. This makes ordinary partial derivatives, directional derivatives, and differential equations available near $x$.

Many natural spaces do not have this global form. The unit sphere

$$S^2 = \left\lbrace (x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1 \right\rbrace$$

is not an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$. It is nevertheless two-dimensional: near every point, two independent parameters describe all nearby points of the sphere.

This reveals two notions of dimension:

  • the ambient dimension, which is the dimension of a surrounding space in a particular realization;
  • the intrinsic dimension, which is the number of coordinates needed locally on the space itself.

For $S^2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$, the ambient dimension is three, whereas the intrinsic dimension is two. Manifold theory is built around intrinsic dimension. In fact, a manifold need not be presented as a subset of any Euclidean space at all.

The basic strategy is simple:

  1. cover the space by coordinate neighborhoods;
  2. identify each neighborhood with an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$;
  3. use ordinary calculus in each coordinate representation;
  4. require that overlapping representations be related by smooth changes of coordinates.

The rest of the theory makes these four steps precise.

2. The topological foundation#

Differentiability presupposes continuity, and continuity presupposes a topology. We therefore begin one level below smooth geometry.

2.1 Topological spaces and continuous maps#

A topology on a set $M$ is a collection $\mathcal{T}$ of subsets of $M$, called open sets, satisfying:

  1. $\varnothing \in \mathcal{T}$ and $M \in \mathcal{T}$;
  2. an arbitrary union of members of $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$;
  3. a finite intersection of members of $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.

The pair $(M, \mathcal{T})$ is a topological space. Usually the topology is understood and we simply write $M$.

A map $F : M \to N$ between topological spaces is continuous if

$$F^{-1}(V)\text{ is open in }M\text{ whenever }V\text{ is open in }N.$$

A homeomorphism is a bijection $F : M \to N$ such that both $F$ and $F^{-1}$ are continuous. Homeomorphic spaces have the same topology: they may look different as subsets of larger spaces, but their neighborhoods and continuous maps have the same structure.

2.2 Topological manifolds#

Definition (topological manifold). Let $d$ be a nonnegative integer. A topological space $M$ is a topological manifold of dimension $d$ if it is locally Euclidean of dimension $d$, Hausdorff, and second countable.

Let us unpack the three conditions.

  1. Locally Euclidean of dimension $d$. For every $p \in M$, there are an open neighborhood $U \subseteq M$ of $p$ and a homeomorphism $\varphi : U \to \varphi(U)$, where $\varphi(U)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^d$.

  2. Hausdorff. If $p, q \in M$ and $p \neq q$, there are disjoint open sets $U, V \subseteq M$ such that $p \in U$ and $q \in V$.

  3. Second countable. There is a countable family of open sets $\mathcal{B} = \left\lbrace B_1, B_2, \ldots \right\rbrace$ such that every open subset of $M$ is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$.

The Hausdorff condition guarantees, among other things, uniqueness of limits. Second countability rules out spaces that are locally reasonable but globally too large for standard analysis. A Hausdorff, second-countable topological manifold is paracompact; after a smooth structure is chosen, this makes smooth partitions of unity available. These global consequences are developed carefully by Lee (2011) and Lee (2012, Chapter 2).

The dimension $d$ is intrinsic. By invariance of domain, nonempty open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^d$ and $\mathbb{R}^m$ cannot be homeomorphic when $d \neq m$. Thus the local dimension cannot change from chart to chart; see Lee (2011).

At this stage, $M$ supports topology but not yet calculus. A homeomorphism need not be differentiable, so we must specify which local coordinate systems are smoothly compatible.

3. Charts: local coordinate systems#

Definition (coordinate chart). A coordinate chart on a topological $d$-manifold $M$ is a pair $(U, \varphi)$ in which $U \subseteq M$ is open and $\varphi : U \to \varphi(U)$ is a homeomorphism onto an open subset $\varphi(U) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$.

The set $U$ is the coordinate neighborhood, and $\varphi$ is the coordinate map. If

$$\varphi(p) = \bigl(x^1(p), \ldots, x^d(p)\bigr),$$

then $p$ is a point of $U$, and the real numbers $x^1(p), \ldots, x^d(p)$ are its coordinate values. To define the coordinate functions precisely, let

$$\operatorname{pr}_i : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad \operatorname{pr}_i(u^1, \ldots, u^d) = u^i,$$

where $\operatorname{pr}_i$ is the projection onto the $i$-th component. The abbreviation $\operatorname{pr}$ stands for “projection,” the subscript $i$ selects the component, and $u = (u^1, \ldots, u^d)$ is an arbitrary point of $\mathbb{R}^d$. For example,

$$\operatorname{pr}_2(u^1, u^2, u^3) = u^2.$$

The $i$-th coordinate function is therefore the composition

$$x^i = \operatorname{pr}_i \circ \varphi : U \to \mathbb{R}.$$

First $\varphi$ turns $p$ into the coordinate tuple $\varphi(p)$; then $\operatorname{pr}_i$ extracts its $i$-th entry. Thus $x^1, \ldots, x^d$ are the coordinate functions, while the ordered tuple $(x^1(p), \ldots, x^d(p))$ gives the coordinates of the particular point $p$.

The inverse map

$$\varphi^{-1} : \varphi(U) \to U$$

can be regarded as a local parameterization of the manifold. The coordinate map assigns numbers to points; its inverse turns coordinate values back into points.

It is useful to keep four related notions separate:

  • $(U, \varphi)$ is the chart;
  • $\varphi$ is the coordinate map;
  • $x^1, \ldots, x^d$ are the coordinate functions;
  • $x^1(p), \ldots, x^d(p)$ are the coordinate values of $p$.

In informal writing, “choose coordinates $(x^1, \ldots, x^d)$” usually means “choose a chart whose component functions are $(x^1, \ldots, x^d)$.”

3.1 Why the chart image must be open#

The requirement that $\varphi(U)$ be open in $\mathbb{R}^d$ ensures that ordinary multivariable calculus applies at every coordinate point. For each $x \in \varphi(U)$, a sufficiently small Euclidean ball around $x$ remains in the coordinate domain.

If chart images were allowed to have arbitrary boundaries or corners, the local differential calculus would depend on the shape of those images. Manifolds with boundary are handled by changing the local model in a controlled way; we return to them in Section 11.

4. Overlapping charts and smooth structures#

A manifold generally requires more than one chart. The crucial question is then: when do two coordinate systems describe the same smooth geometry?

4.1 Why one chart may not suffice#

The Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^d$ has the global chart $(\mathbb{R}^d, \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{R}^d})$. The circle

$$S^1 = \left\lbrace (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1 \right\rbrace$$

has no global chart taking it onto an open subset of $\mathbb{R}$. Indeed, $S^1$ is compact, whereas every nonempty open subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is noncompact. Because compactness is preserved by homeomorphisms, such a chart cannot exist.

Locally, however, one coordinate is enough. On the upper semicircle

$$U_+ = \left\lbrace (x, y) \in S^1 : y \gt 0 \right\rbrace,$$

the map

$$\varphi_+(x, y) = x$$

is a chart with inverse

$$\varphi_+^{-1}(u) = \left(u, \sqrt{1 - u^2}\right), \qquad -1 \lt u \lt 1.$$

Other charts cover the parts omitted by $U_+$. The circle is therefore locally, but not globally, an open subset of $\mathbb{R}$.

4.2 Transition maps#

Let $(U, \varphi)$ and $(V, \psi)$ be charts with $U \cap V \neq \varnothing$. A point $p \in U \cap V$ has two coordinate descriptions:

$$x = \varphi(p), \qquad y = \psi(p).$$

The change from $x$-coordinates to $y$-coordinates is the transition map

$$\psi \circ \varphi^{-1} : \varphi(U \cap V) \to \psi(U \cap V).$$

Both its domain and codomain are open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^d$, so ordinary Euclidean differentiability applies.

Definition (smooth compatibility). Two charts are smoothly compatible if their domains are disjoint or their transition map is a $C^\infty$ diffeomorphism.

Because the transition map is bijective, this means that both $\psi \circ \varphi^{-1}$ and its inverse $\varphi \circ \psi^{-1}$ are infinitely differentiable.

Figure 1. Two charts turn an overlap $U \cap V$ on the manifold into two Euclidean coordinate regions. The transition maps $\psi \circ \varphi^{-1}$ and $\varphi \circ \psi^{-1}$ translate between those regions. Figure by Stomatapoll, licensed under CC BY-SA 3.0.

In coordinates, smooth compatibility says that the functions

$$y^a = y^a(x^1, \ldots, x^d), \qquad a = 1, \ldots, d,$$

and the inverse functions

$$x^i = x^i(y^1, \ldots, y^d), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, d,$$

are smooth. The Jacobian matrices of these transformations are inverse at every point of the overlap.

4.3 Atlases and maximal atlases#

Definition (smooth atlas). A smooth atlas on $M$ is a collection $\mathcal{A} = \left\lbrace (U_\alpha, \varphi_\alpha) \right\rbrace_{\alpha \in I}$ of pairwise smoothly compatible charts whose domains cover $M$:

$$M = \bigcup_{\alpha \in I}U_\alpha.$$

The symbol $\mathcal{A}$ names the atlas, $I$ is an arbitrary index set, and $\alpha \in I$ is only a label selecting one chart $(U_\alpha, \varphi_\alpha)$. The union condition means that every point of $M$ belongs to at least one coordinate neighborhood $U_\alpha$.

Two smooth atlases are equivalent if every chart in one is smoothly compatible with every chart in the other. Equivalent atlases define the same notion of smoothness.

Every smooth atlas is contained in a unique maximal smooth atlas: the collection of all charts smoothly compatible with the original atlas. “Maximal” does not mean that the individual chart domains are as large as possible. It means that no additional compatible chart has been left out.

Definition (smooth manifold). A smooth $d$-manifold is a topological $d$-manifold equipped with a maximal smooth atlas.

In practice, one almost never writes down the maximal atlas. A convenient atlas is specified, and the compatible maximal atlas is understood.

This construction is standard; Lee (2012, Chapter 1) also explains why specifying any smooth atlas determines a unique maximal atlas. The definition may be summarized as

$$\boxed{\text{smooth manifold} = \text{topological manifold} + \text{smoothly compatible local coordinates}.}$$

The topology determines which neighborhoods and maps are continuous. The smooth structure determines which functions and coordinate changes are differentiable.

5. Fundamental examples and nonexamples#

Examples reveal what the definition permits—and what it rules out.

5.1 Euclidean spaces and open subsets#

The identity chart gives $\mathbb{R}^d$ its standard smooth structure:

$$\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{R}^d} : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d.$$

Every open subset $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ is likewise a smooth $d$-manifold with the restricted identity chart.

5.2 The sphere#

For $d \geq 1$, define

$$S^d = \left\lbrace x = (x^1, \ldots, x^{d + 1}) \in \mathbb{R}^{d + 1} : \sum_{i = 1}^{d + 1} (x^i)^2 = 1 \right\rbrace.$$

Let

$$N = (0, \ldots, 0, 1), \qquad S = (0, \ldots, 0, -1)$$

be the north and south poles, and set $U_N = S^d \setminus \left\lbrace N \right\rbrace$ and $U_S = S^d \setminus \left\lbrace S \right\rbrace$. Stereographic projection defines charts

$$\varphi_N(x) = \frac{(x^1, \ldots, x^d)}{1 - x^{d + 1}}, \qquad \varphi_S(x) = \frac{(x^1, \ldots, x^d)}{1 + x^{d + 1}}.$$

Both maps take values in $\mathbb{R}^d$. On the overlap, if $u = \varphi_N(x) \neq 0$, then

$$\bigl(\varphi_S \circ \varphi_N^{-1}\bigr)(u) = \frac{u}{\lVert u \rVert^2}.$$

Here $u \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the coordinate vector supplied by the north-pole chart, and $\lVert u \rVert = \sqrt{\sum_{i = 1}^d (u^i)^2}$ is its Euclidean norm. The value $u = 0$ is excluded because it represents the south pole in the north-pole chart, and the south-pole chart is not defined there. This transition map is smooth on $\mathbb{R}^d \setminus \left\lbrace 0 \right\rbrace$ and is its own inverse. Hence the two stereographic charts form a smooth atlas on $S^d$. Because each chart uses $d$ coordinates, $\dim S^d = d$.

5.3 Products and tori#

If $M$ and $N$ are smooth manifolds of dimensions $m$ and $n$, their product has a natural smooth structure of dimension $m + n$. Product charts are given by

$$(U \times V, \varphi \times \psi), \qquad (\varphi \times \psi)(p, q) = (\varphi(p), \psi(q)).$$

Here $U \subseteq M$ and $V \subseteq N$ are coordinate neighborhoods; $(p, q)$ is an ordered pair with $p \in U$ and $q \in V$; and $\varphi \times \psi$ is the product coordinate map. The canonical product projections are

$$\operatorname{pr}_1 : M \times N \to M, \qquad \operatorname{pr}_1(p, q) = p,$$

and

$$\operatorname{pr}_2 : M \times N \to N, \qquad \operatorname{pr}_2(p, q) = q.$$

These maps have the same “select one component” meaning as the coordinate projection $\operatorname{pr}_i$ introduced in Section 3, but their domains are now products of manifolds.

In particular,

$$T^d = (S^1)^d$$

is a smooth $d$-manifold. Here $T$ stands for torus, and $(S^1)^d$ means the $d$-fold Cartesian product $S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1$, not an ordinary numerical power. The two-dimensional torus $T^2 = S^1 \times S^1$ may be visualized as the familiar doughnut-shaped surface, but its product definition is intrinsic and does not depend on an embedding in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

5.4 Matrix groups#

Identify the vector space of real $n \times n$ matrices with $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$. The notation $\det A$ means the determinant of the square matrix $A$. Since the determinant is continuous,

$$GL(n, \mathbb{R}) = \left\lbrace A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} : \det A \neq 0 \right\rbrace$$

is open in $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$. The letters $GL$ abbreviate general linear group: $GL(n, \mathbb{R})$ is precisely the set of invertible real $n \times n$ matrices. It is therefore a smooth manifold of dimension $n^2$.

5.5 A crossing is not a manifold#

Consider the union of the two coordinate axes,

$$X = \left\lbrace (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : xy = 0 \right\rbrace.$$

Away from the origin, $X$ locally resembles an open interval. At the origin, however, intersecting $X$ with any sufficiently small Euclidean ball gives a connected cross whose punctured version has four connected components. If the origin had a one-dimensional manifold chart, we could restrict it to a connected coordinate neighborhood; its image would be an open interval in $\mathbb{R}$. Removing the chart image of the origin from that interval would produce at most two connected components, contradicting the four local branches of the cross. Therefore $X$ is not a one-dimensional manifold.

The failure is topological, not merely a lack of smoothness. No choice of coordinates can repair the branching at the origin.

6. Smooth functions and smooth maps#

Charts allow Euclidean calculus to be transported to an abstract manifold.

6.1 Smooth real-valued functions#

Let $M$ be a smooth manifold. A function $f : M \to \mathbb{R}$ is smooth at $p \in M$ if there is a chart $(U, \varphi)$ containing $p$ such that

$$f \circ \varphi^{-1} : \varphi(U) \to \mathbb{R}$$

is smooth in the ordinary Euclidean sense. The function is smooth on $M$ if it is smooth at every point.

This condition is independent of the chosen chart. If $(V, \psi)$ is another chart at $p$, then on the overlap

$$f \circ \psi^{-1} = (f \circ \varphi^{-1}) \circ (\varphi \circ \psi^{-1}).$$

The first map on the right is smooth by assumption, and the second is a smooth transition map. Their composition is smooth. This small calculation is the basic mechanism behind coordinate independence throughout differential geometry.

The vector space—and, under pointwise multiplication, commutative algebra—of smooth real-valued functions is denoted by $C^\infty(M)$.

6.2 Smooth maps between manifolds#

Let $M$ and $N$ be smooth manifolds, and let $F : M \to N$ be a map. Choose a chart $(U, \varphi)$ on $M$ and a chart $(V, \psi)$ on $N$. Wherever $F(U)$ meets $V$, the coordinate representation is

$$\psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1} : \varphi\bigl(U \cap F^{-1}(V)\bigr) \to \psi(V).$$

Definition (smooth map). The map $F$ is smooth if it is continuous and every such local coordinate representation is a smooth map between open subsets of Euclidean spaces.

The continuity assumption is redundant if smooth coordinate representations are required around every point, because those local representations are continuous. Including it here makes the logical order transparent. The chart-based definitions in this section agree with the intrinsic definitions in Lee (2012, Chapter 2) and Tu (2011, Chapters 2 and 3).

A bijective smooth map whose inverse is smooth is a diffeomorphism. Diffeomorphic manifolds are equivalent as smooth manifolds. A homeomorphism preserves topology; a diffeomorphism preserves topology together with the smooth structure.

6.3 Smooth curves#

A smooth curve in $M$ is a smooth map

$$\gamma : I \to M,$$

where $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is an open interval. In a chart, the curve becomes

$$(\varphi \circ \gamma)(t) = \bigl(x^1(t), \ldots, x^d(t)\bigr).$$

The coordinate functions $x^i(t)$ can be differentiated by ordinary calculus. Their derivatives will lead us to the intrinsic notion of a tangent vector.

7. Tangent vectors: intrinsic velocities#

The tangent line to a plane curve or the tangent plane to a surface is usually drawn inside an ambient Euclidean space. An abstract manifold may have no preferred ambient space, so its tangent vectors must be defined intrinsically.

There are two complementary descriptions:

  1. a tangent vector is the velocity of a curve through a point;
  2. a tangent vector is a derivation that differentiates smooth functions at that point.

The curve description supplies the geometry. The derivation description captures exactly what the velocity does to observable functions.

7.1 Tangent vectors as equivalence classes of curves#

Fix $p \in M$. Let $\mathcal{C}_p$ be the collection of smooth curves $\gamma : (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to M$ satisfying $\gamma(0) = p$, where $\varepsilon \gt 0$ may depend on the curve.

Choose a chart $(U, \varphi)$ around $p$. Two curves $\gamma_1, \gamma_2 \in \mathcal{C}_p$ are declared equivalent if

$$\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\rvert_{t = 0}(\varphi \circ \gamma_1)(t) = \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\rvert_{t = 0}(\varphi \circ \gamma_2)(t).$$

The symbol $t$ is the real curve parameter, $\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t$ means ordinary differentiation with respect to $t$, and the vertical evaluation bar with subscript $t = 0$ means “differentiate first, then set $t$ equal to zero.” This relation does not depend on the chart. If $(V, \psi)$ is another chart around $p$, then

$$\psi \circ \gamma = (\psi \circ \varphi^{-1}) \circ (\varphi \circ \gamma),$$

and the Euclidean chain rule gives

$$\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\rvert_{0}(\psi \circ \gamma)(t) = D(\psi \circ \varphi^{-1})_{\varphi(p)}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\rvert_{0}(\varphi \circ \gamma)(t).$$

Equal velocities in $\varphi$-coordinates therefore remain equal in $\psi$-coordinates.

Definition (tangent vector). A tangent vector at $p$ is an equivalence class $[\gamma]$ of curves through $p$ under the preceding relation.

The tangent vector represented by $\gamma$ is denoted by $\dot{\gamma}(0)$ or $\gamma^\prime(0)$; the dot and prime are two notations for differentiation with respect to the curve parameter. The collection of all tangent vectors at $p$ is the tangent space $T_pM$.

7.2 The vector-space structure and coordinate basis#

The chart $(U, \varphi)$ identifies $T_pM$ with $\mathbb{R}^d$ through

$$[\gamma] \longmapsto \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\rvert_{0}(\varphi \circ \gamma)(t).$$

We use this identification to add tangent vectors and multiply them by scalars. Because changes of coordinates act through invertible linear Jacobian maps, the resulting vector-space structure is independent of the selected chart. Consequently,

$$\dim T_pM = d.$$

For sufficiently small $t$, define

$$\gamma_i(t) = \varphi^{-1}\bigl(\varphi(p) + t e_i\bigr),$$

where $e_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector of $\mathbb{R}^d$. The tangent vector $\dot{\gamma}_i(0)$ is denoted by

$$\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right\rvert_p.$$

The vectors

$$\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^1}\right\rvert_p, \ldots, \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^d}\right\rvert_p$$

form the coordinate basis of $T_pM$. Every $v \in T_pM$ has a unique representation

$$\boxed{v = \sum_{i = 1}^d v^i \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right\rvert_p.}$$

If $v = \dot{\gamma}(0)$, then

$$v^i = \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\rvert_0 x^i(\gamma(t)).$$

The numbers $v^i$ are components of the vector relative to the coordinate basis; they are not the vector itself.

7.3 Tangent vectors as derivations#

Let $C_p^\infty(M)$ denote the algebra of germs of smooth functions at $p$. Informally, two functions determine the same germ if they agree on some neighborhood of $p$. A derivation at $p$ is a linear map

$$v : C_p^\infty(M) \to \mathbb{R}$$

satisfying the Leibniz rule

$$v[fg] = f(p)v[g] + g(p)v[f].$$

A curve velocity defines a derivation by

$$\dot{\gamma}(0)[f] = \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\rvert_0 f(\gamma(t)).$$

This depends only on the germ of $f$ near $p$. In local coordinates, if $v = \sum_i v^i \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right\rvert_p$, then

$$\boxed{v[f] = \sum_{i = 1}^d v^i \frac{\partial(f \circ \varphi^{-1})}{\partial x^i}\bigl(\varphi(p)\bigr).}$$

Conversely, every derivation at $p$ has this form. To see the decisive step, write $a = \varphi(p)$ and $h = f \circ \varphi^{-1}$. On a sufficiently small coordinate neighborhood, the smooth Hadamard formula gives

$$h(u) - h(a) = \sum_{i = 1}^d (u^i - a^i) h_i(u), \qquad h_i(a) = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x^i}(a),$$

where $u = (u^1, \ldots, u^d)$ is a variable coordinate point near $a$, and the $h_i$ are auxiliary smooth functions supplied by Hadamard’s formula. Applying the derivation and using the Leibniz rule shows that its action on $f$ is determined by its action on the coordinate functions. The coefficients are

$$v^i = v[x^i],$$

and substitution yields the displayed directional-derivative formula. Thus curve velocities and derivations define canonically isomorphic versions of $T_pM$. This equivalence is treated in detail by Lee (2012, Chapter 3) and Tu (2011, Chapter 3).

The derivation viewpoint immediately implies that tangent vectors annihilate constants:

$$v[c] = 0\qquad\text{for every constant }c.$$

7.4 Coordinate transformation law#

Suppose $x = (x^1, \ldots, x^d)$ and $y = (y^1, \ldots, y^d)$ are two coordinate systems around $p$. The chain rule gives

$$\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right\rvert_p = \sum_{a = 1}^d \frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^i}(p) \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial y^a}\right\rvert_p.$$

Both $i$ and $a$ range from $1$ to $d$; two letters are used so that an $x$-coordinate label cannot be confused with a $y$-coordinate label.

If

$$v = \sum_{i = 1}^d v_x^i \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i} = \sum_{a = 1}^d v_y^a \frac{\partial}{\partial y^a},$$

then

$$\boxed{v_y^a = \sum_{i = 1}^d \frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^i}(p) v_x^i.}$$

In matrix notation,

$$[v]_y = D(y \circ x^{-1})_{x(p)} [v]_x.$$

Here $\lbrack v\rbrack_x$ and $\lbrack v\rbrack_y$ are the component columns of the same tangent vector in the $x$- and $y$-coordinate bases, respectively. The symbol $D(y \circ x^{-1})_{x(p)}$ denotes the Jacobian matrix of the transition map at the coordinate point $x(p)$. The components transform by this Jacobian, while the geometric vector is unchanged.

7.5 Running example: the tangent space of the sphere#

Let $p \in S^2$ and let $\gamma$ be a curve in $S^2$ with $\gamma(0) = p$. Because $\gamma(t) \cdot \gamma(t) = 1$, differentiation at $t = 0$ gives

$$2p \cdot \dot{\gamma}(0) = 0.$$

Every tangent vector $v = \dot{\gamma}(0)$ is therefore orthogonal to $p$. Conversely, if $v \in \mathbb{R}^3$ satisfies $p \cdot v = 0$ and $v \neq 0$, then

$$\gamma(t) = \cos(\lVert v \rVert t) p + \frac{\sin(\lVert v \rVert t)}{\lVert v \rVert} v$$

lies on $S^2$, satisfies $\gamma(0) = p$, and has $\dot{\gamma}(0) = v$. For $v = 0$, use the constant curve. Hence

$$\boxed{T_pS^2 = \left\lbrace v \in \mathbb{R}^3 : p \cdot v = 0 \right\rbrace.}$$

The intrinsic tangent space is thus naturally identified with the ordinary plane through the origin perpendicular to $p$.

8. Differentials and the tangent bundle#

Tangent vectors make it possible to differentiate maps between manifolds.

8.1 The differential of a smooth map#

Let $F : M \to N$ be smooth and let $v = [\gamma] \in T_pM$. Define

$$dF_p(v) = [F \circ \gamma] \in T_{F(p)}N.$$

This is well defined: if two curves have the same velocity at $p$, the chain rule shows that their images under $F$ have the same velocity at $F(p)$. The resulting map

$$dF_p : T_pM \to T_{F(p)}N$$

is linear and is called the differential, pushforward, or tangent map of $F$ at $p$.

Choose coordinates $x = (x^1, \ldots, x^d)$ on $M$ and $y = (y^1, \ldots, y^m)$ on $N$. If

$$F^\alpha = y^\alpha \circ F \circ x^{-1},$$

then

$$dF_p\left(\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right\rvert_p\right) = \sum_{\alpha = 1}^m \frac{\partial F^\alpha}{\partial x^i}(x(p)) \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial y^\alpha}\right\rvert_{F(p)}.$$

Thus $dF_p$ is represented by the Jacobian matrix

$$DF(x(p)) = \left(\frac{\partial F^\alpha}{\partial x^i}(x(p))\right)_{\alpha, i}.$$

The function $F^\alpha$ is the $\alpha$-th coordinate component of $F$, the target index $\alpha$ ranges from $1$ to $m$, and the source index $i$ ranges from $1$ to $d$. The subscript $(\alpha, i)$ means that the displayed derivative occupies row $\alpha$ and column $i$ of the matrix. The notation $DF(x(p))$ is shorthand for the Jacobian of the coordinate representation $y \circ F \circ x^{-1}$ at $x(p)$.

For smooth maps $F : M \to N$ and $G : N \to P$, the chain rule becomes the intrinsic identity

$$\boxed{d(G \circ F)_p = dG_{F(p)} \circ dF_p.}$$

When $F$ is a diffeomorphism, $dF_p$ is a linear isomorphism and

$$d(F^{-1})_{F(p)} = (dF_p)^{-1}.$$

8.2 The tangent bundle as a manifold#

The tangent spaces at all points are assembled into the tangent bundle

$$TM = \bigsqcup_{p \in M} T_pM.$$

The notation $\bigsqcup$ means disjoint union: even when two tangent vectors have the same coordinate components, they are different elements of $TM$ if they are based at different points. An element of $TM$ is written $(p, v)$ with $p \in M$ and $v \in T_pM$. The bundle projection is

$$\pi : TM \to M, \qquad \pi(p, v) = p.$$

Thus $\pi$ forgets the vector and remembers its base point. The set $\pi^{-1}(U)$ consists of all tangent vectors based at points of $U$. A chart $(U, \varphi)$ on $M$ induces a chart on $\pi^{-1}(U) \subseteq TM$:

$$T\varphi : \pi^{-1}(U) \to \varphi(U) \times \mathbb{R}^d, \qquad T\varphi(p, v) = \bigl(\varphi(p), [v]_\varphi\bigr).$$

Here $T\varphi$ is the tangent-bundle chart induced by $\varphi$, and $[v]_\varphi = (v^1, \ldots, v^d)$ is the coordinate-component vector of $v$ in the basis determined by $\varphi$. The letter $T$ in $T\varphi$ signals that the chart acts simultaneously on base points and tangent vectors; it is not a matrix transpose.

If $y = y(x)$ is a change of coordinates on $M$, the corresponding change of tangent-bundle coordinates is

$$(x, v_x) \longmapsto \bigl(y(x), D y(x) v_x\bigr).$$

In this formula, $x$ denotes the old coordinate vector of the base point, $y(x)$ denotes its new coordinate vector, $v_x$ denotes the old component column of the tangent vector, and $D y(x)$ is the Jacobian matrix of the coordinate change at $x$. This map is smooth because $y$ is smooth. These induced charts give $TM$ a natural smooth structure of dimension $2d$:

$$\dim TM = 2d.$$

The first $d$ coordinates locate the base point, and the remaining $d$ coordinates record the tangent-vector components. This bundle construction, including its coordinate changes, is standard; see Lee (2012, Chapter 3).

9. Cotangent vectors, differentials, and one-forms#

A tangent vector is an infinitesimal direction. A cotangent vector is a linear measuring device: it accepts a tangent vector and returns a number.

9.1 Cotangent spaces and the dual basis#

Definition (cotangent space). The cotangent space at $p$ is the dual vector space

$$\boxed{T_p^\ast M = \operatorname{Hom}(T_pM, \mathbb{R}).}$$

Here $\operatorname{Hom}(T_pM, \mathbb{R})$ denotes the vector space of all real-linear maps from $T_pM$ to $\mathbb{R}$. The superscript $\ast$ is the symbol for the algebraic dual. An element $\alpha_p \in T_p^\ast M$ is a cotangent vector or covector. The Greek letter $\alpha$ names the covector, while the subscript $p$ records its base point. Its evaluation on $v \in T_pM$ is the canonical pairing

$$\langle \alpha_p, v \rangle = \alpha_p(v).$$

The angle brackets and the expression $\alpha_p(v)$ mean the same thing: apply the linear functional $\alpha_p$ to the vector $v$. This pairing requires no metric. Since $\dim T_pM = d$, we also have $\dim T_p^\ast M = d$.

The coordinate basis $\left\lbrace \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right\rvert_p \right\rbrace_{i = 1}^d$ determines the dual basis $\left\lbrace \lparen dx^i\rparen_p \right\rbrace_{i=1}^d$, characterized by

$$(dx^i)_p\left(\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\right\rvert_p\right) = \delta^i_j.$$

The symbol $\delta^i_j$ is the Kronecker delta: it equals $1$ when $i = j$ and $0$ when $i \neq j$. Thus $(dx^i)_p$ returns $1$ on the $i$-th coordinate basis vector and $0$ on all the others.

Every covector has a unique expansion

$$\boxed{\alpha_p = \sum_{i = 1}^d \alpha_i (dx^i)_p.}$$

If $v = \sum_i v^i \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right\rvert_p$, then

$$\alpha_p(v) = \sum_{i = 1}^d \alpha_i v^i.$$

This row-by-column expression is merely the coordinate form of the intrinsic pairing.

9.2 The differential of a function#

For $f \in C^\infty(M)$, the differential of $f$ at $p$ is the covector $df_p \in T_p^\ast M$ defined by

$$\boxed{df_p(v) = v[f].}$$

If $v = \dot{\gamma}(0)$, then

$$df_p(v) = \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\rvert_0 f(\gamma(t)).$$

Thus $df_p$ records the first-order rate of change of $f$ in every possible tangent direction. In local coordinates,

$$\boxed{df_p = \sum_{i = 1}^d \frac{\partial(f \circ \varphi^{-1})}{\partial x^i}\bigl(\varphi(p)\bigr) (dx^i)_p.}$$

The notation $dx^i$ is literal: $dx^i$ is the differential of the coordinate function $x^i$. It extracts the corresponding vector component:

$$(dx^i)_p(v) = v[x^i] = v^i.$$

For example, on $\mathbb{R}^2$, let $f(x, y) = x^2y + \sin y$. Then

$$df = 2xy\,dx + (x^2 + \cos y)\,dy.$$

For $v = a \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + b \frac{\partial}{\partial y}$,

$$df(v) = 2axy + b(x^2 + \cos y).$$

The covector $df$ is not itself a direction. It is the rule that measures the change of $f$ in a supplied direction.

9.3 Coordinate transformation law for covectors#

For two coordinate systems $x = (x^1, \ldots, x^d)$ and $y = (y^1, \ldots, y^d)$, the chain rule gives

$$dy^a = \sum_{i = 1}^d \frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i, \qquad dx^i = \sum_{a = 1}^d \frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}\,dy^a.$$

If

$$\alpha = \sum_{i = 1}^d \alpha_i^x\,dx^i = \sum_{a = 1}^d \alpha_a^y\,dy^a,$$

then

$$\boxed{\alpha_a^y = \sum_{i = 1}^d \frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a} \alpha_i^x.}$$

The number $\alpha_i^x$ is the $i$-th component of $\alpha$ in the $x$-dual basis, while $\alpha_a^y$ is its $a$-th component in the $y$-dual basis. The superscripts $x$ and $y$ here are coordinate labels, not powers.

In matrix form,

$$[\alpha]_y = D(x \circ y^{-1})_{y(p)}^T [\alpha]_x.$$

Here $[\alpha]_x$ and $[\alpha]_y$ are the component columns of the same covector in the two coordinate systems, and the superscript $T$ denotes matrix transpose. Vectors transform by the Jacobian of $y$ with respect to $x$, whereas covector components transform by the transpose of the inverse Jacobian. These laws guarantee

$$\alpha(v) = \sum_i \alpha_i^x v_x^i = \sum_a \alpha_a^y v_y^a.$$

The scalar pairing is therefore independent of coordinates.

9.4 The cotangent bundle and one-forms#

The cotangent spaces form the cotangent bundle

$$T^\ast M = \bigsqcup_{p \in M} T_p^\ast M.$$

Using coordinates $(x^1, \ldots, x^d)$ on $M$ and covector components $(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_d)$ gives local coordinates

$$(x^1, \ldots, x^d, \alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_d)$$

on $T^\ast M$. Hence

$$\dim T^\ast M = 2d.$$

Let $\pi_{T^\ast M} : T^\ast M \to M$ be the cotangent-bundle projection, defined by $\pi_{T^\ast M}(p, \alpha_p) = p$. A smooth map $\alpha : M \to T^\ast M$ satisfying $\pi_{T^\ast M} \circ \alpha = \operatorname{id}_M$ is a smooth one-form. This identity says that $\alpha(p)$ belongs to the cotangent space based at the same point $p$. Locally,

$$\alpha = \sum_{i = 1}^d \alpha_i(x)\,dx^i,$$

where all coefficient functions $\alpha_i$ are smooth. The one-form $df$ is called exact.

9.5 Pullbacks#

Let $F : M \to N$ be smooth. The differential moves tangent vectors forward:

$$dF_p : T_pM \to T_{F(p)}N.$$

Duality moves covectors in the opposite direction. If $\beta_{F(p)} \in T_{F(p)}^\ast N$, define

$$(dF_p)^\ast\beta_{F(p)} \in T_p^\ast M$$

by

$$\bigl((dF_p)^\ast\beta_{F(p)}\bigr)(v) = \beta_{F(p)}\bigl(dF_p(v)\bigr).$$

Here $(dF_p)^\ast$ is the dual linear map of $dF_p$: it converts a covector at $F(p)$ into a covector at $p$ by precomposition with $dF_p$. If $\beta$ is a one-form on $N$, its pullback $F^\ast\beta$ is the one-form on $M$ given by

$$\boxed{(F^\ast\beta)_p = (dF_p)^\ast\beta_{F(p)}.}$$

For a smooth function $f : N \to \mathbb{R}$, the chain rule is equivalently

$$d(f \circ F) = F^\ast(df).$$

9.6 Why $df$ is not automatically a gradient#

In Euclidean calculus, $df$ and $\nabla f$ are often identified because the standard inner product is silently available; here $\nabla f$ denotes the usual Euclidean gradient vector. On an abstract smooth manifold, they belong to different spaces:

$$df_p \in T_p^\ast M, \qquad \operatorname{grad}f(p) \in T_pM.$$

The smooth structure supplies $df$, but it supplies no canonical isomorphism $T_p^\ast M \to T_pM$. If a Riemannian metric $g$ is added, then $g_p$ is an inner product on $T_pM$, and the gradient is the unique tangent vector defined by

$$g_p(\operatorname{grad}f, v) = df_p(v)\qquad\text{for every }v \in T_pM.$$

If $(g^{ij})$ is the inverse of the coordinate matrix $(g_{ij})$, then

$$\operatorname{grad}f = \sum_{i, j = 1}^d g^{ij} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^j} \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}.$$

Here $g_{ij} = g\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\right)$ are the coordinate coefficients of the metric, and the numbers $g^{ij}$ are defined by the inverse-matrix relation $\sum_{j = 1}^d g_{ij}g^{jk} = \delta_i^k$. They are not obtained by numerically exponentiating $g_{ij}$.

The distinction between $df$ and $\operatorname{grad}f$ is not cosmetic: it separates what follows from smooth structure alone from what depends on a chosen metric. The dual-space, pullback, and one-form constructions in this section follow Lee (2012, Chapters 3 and 11) and Tu (2011, Chapters 3 and 5).

10. Vector fields and differential equations#

A single tangent vector gives one infinitesimal direction at one point. A vector field chooses such a direction smoothly at every point and thereby prescribes a differential equation on the manifold.

10.1 Vector fields as sections#

Definition (smooth vector field). A smooth vector field is a smooth map $X : M \to TM$ satisfying

$$\pi \circ X = \operatorname{id}_M.$$

The symbol $X$ names the field, and $\pi : TM \to M$ is the tangent-bundle projection from Section 8. The identity $\pi(X(p)) = p$ says that the chosen vector is based at the point where the field is evaluated. Equivalently, $X(p) = X_p \in T_pM$ for each $p$. In local coordinates,

$$\boxed{X = \sum_{i = 1}^d X^i(x) \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}.}$$

The scalar function $X^i$ is the $i$-th coordinate component of the field, and $x = (x^1, \ldots, x^d)$ denotes the local coordinate vector. The field $X$ is smooth precisely when every component function $X^i$ is smooth.

If $y = y(x)$ is another coordinate system, then

$$X_y^a = \sum_{i = 1}^d \frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^i} X_x^i.$$

The symbols $X_x^i$ and $X_y^a$ denote the components of the same field in the $x$- and $y$-coordinate bases. The components change, but the section $X$ does not.

10.2 Vector fields as derivations#

A vector field acts on $f \in C^\infty(M)$ pointwise:

$$X[f](p) = X_p[f].$$

In coordinates,

$$X[f] = \sum_{i = 1}^d X^i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}.$$

It is real-linear and obeys

$$X[fg] = f X[g] + g X[f].$$

Conversely, every real-linear derivation $D : C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ is induced by a unique smooth vector field. Here $D$ is merely the name of an abstract algebra derivation; it should not be confused with the Jacobian notation $D h_a$ used for Euclidean maps. Smooth vector fields can therefore be identified with derivations of the algebra $C^\infty(M)$.

10.3 Integral curves and local existence#

Let $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be an interval. A smooth curve $\gamma : I \to M$ is an integral curve of $X$ if

$$\boxed{\dot{\gamma}(t) = X_{\gamma(t)}\qquad\text{for every }t \in I.}$$

The initial-value problem through $p \in M$ is

$$\dot{\gamma}(t) = X_{\gamma(t)}, \qquad \gamma(0) = p.$$

In local coordinates $\gamma(t) = (x^1(t), \ldots, x^d(t))$, this becomes

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}x^i}{\mathrm{d}t} = X^i(x^1(t), \ldots, x^d(t)), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, d.$$

Because the component functions are smooth, they are locally Lipschitz. The Euclidean existence-and-uniqueness theorem therefore yields a unique local solution through each initial point. Solutions obtained in different charts agree on overlaps by uniqueness, so they combine into an intrinsic integral curve on $M$.

Each initial point $p$ has a unique maximal integral curve

$$\gamma_p : I_p \to M,$$

where the subscript $p$ records the initial value $\gamma_p(0) = p$, and $I_p \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is the largest open interval containing $0$ on which the solution exists.

10.4 Complete fields and flows#

A vector field is complete if $I_p = \mathbb{R}$ for every $p \in M$. Smoothness alone does not imply completeness. On $\mathbb{R}$,

$$X = x^2 \frac{\partial}{\partial x}$$

has the solution

$$x(t) = \frac{x_0}{1 - x_0 t},$$

where $x_0 = x(0)$ is the initial value. This solution blows up at $t = 1/x_0$ when $x_0 \gt 0$. By contrast, every smooth vector field on a compact manifold without boundary is complete.

The set $\mathcal{D} = \left\lbrace (t, p) \in \mathbb{R} \times M : t \in I_p \right\rbrace$ is open in $\mathbb{R} \times M$. On this domain, set

$$\Phi(t, p) = \gamma_p(t).$$

Here $\mathcal{D}$ is the maximal flow domain, $t$ is the time variable, and $\Phi(t, p)$ is the point reached after flowing for time $t$ from the initial point $p$. The map $\Phi$ is the local flow generated by $X$. It satisfies

$$\Phi(0, p) = p, \qquad \Phi(t, \Phi(s, p)) = \Phi(t + s, p)$$

whenever both sides are defined; $s$ and $t$ are two admissible time increments. The field is recovered from its flow by

$$X_p = \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\rvert_{t = 0}\Phi(t, p).$$

If $X$ is complete, $\Phi$ is defined on all of $\mathbb{R} \times M$. Each time-$t$ map $\Phi_t(p) = \Phi(t, p)$ is a diffeomorphism and

$$\Phi_t^{-1} = \Phi_{-t}.$$

Thus a complete vector field generates a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms. The local existence theorem, smooth dependence, maximal flow domain, and compact-manifold completeness result are proved in Lee (2012, Chapter 9).

10.5 Equilibria and conserved functions#

A point $p$ is an equilibrium if $X_p = 0$. The integral curve through an equilibrium is constant.

Along any integral curve,

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} f(\gamma(t)) = X[f](\gamma(t)).$$

Therefore a smooth function $f$ is constant along every integral curve exactly when

$$\boxed{X[f] = 0.}$$

This condition is intrinsic and requires no coordinates or metric.

10.6 The Lie bracket#

For smooth vector fields $X$ and $Y$, define

$$\boxed{[X, Y][f] = X[Y[f]] - Y[X[f]].}$$

Here $f \in C^\infty(M)$ is an arbitrary smooth test function, square brackets around $X$ and $Y$ denote their Lie bracket, and $X[f]$ denotes differentiation of $f$ along $X$. Although each composition on the right is generally a second-order operator, their second-order terms cancel. The result is a derivation and hence another vector field. In local coordinates,

$$[X, Y] = \sum_{i = 1}^d \left(\sum_{j = 1}^d X^j \frac{\partial Y^i}{\partial x^j} - \sum_{j = 1}^d Y^j \frac{\partial X^i}{\partial x^j}\right) \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}.$$

The Lie bracket is antisymmetric and satisfies the Jacobi identity:

$$[X, Y] = -[Y, X], \qquad [X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = 0.$$

The first identity holds for any two smooth vector fields $X$ and $Y$; the second is the Jacobi identity and holds for any three smooth vector fields $X$, $Y$, and $Z$.

It is not $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear. Instead,

$$[X, fY] = f[X, Y] + X[f] Y.$$

In this identity, $fY$ is the vector field defined by $(fY)_p = f(p)Y_p$, and $X[f]Y$ multiplies $Y$ by the scalar function $X[f]$. Geometrically, $[X, Y]$ measures the infinitesimal failure of the flows of $X$ and $Y$ to commute. The local flows commute wherever both compositions are defined if and only if $[X, Y] = 0$; see Lee (2012, Chapters 8 and 9).

10.7 Running example: rotation on the sphere#

On $S^2$, consider

$$X(x, y, z) = (-y, x, 0).$$

The field is tangent to the sphere because

$$(x, y, z) \cdot (-y, x, 0) = 0.$$

Its integral-curve equations are

$$\dot{x} = -y, \qquad \dot{y} = x, \qquad \dot{z} = 0.$$

Through $(x_0, y_0, z_0) \in S^2$, the solution is

$$x(t) = x_0 \cos t - y_0 \sin t, \qquad y(t) = x_0 \sin t + y_0 \cos t, \qquad z(t) = z_0.$$

The flow rotates the sphere around the $z$-axis. The height function $f(x, y, z) = z$ is conserved because $X[f] = 0$. In this one example, the abstract notions of tangent vector, vector field, integral curve, flow, and first integral become a single familiar motion.

11. Submanifolds and manifolds with boundary#

Not every subset of a manifold is itself a manifold. The subset must sit locally like a linear coordinate slice.

11.1 Embedded submanifolds#

Let $M$ be a smooth $d$-manifold and let $S \subseteq M$. We call $S$ an embedded submanifold of dimension $k$ if, for every $p \in S$, there is a chart $(U, \varphi)$ on $M$ such that

$$\varphi(U \cap S) = \varphi(U) \cap \bigl(\mathbb{R}^k \times \left\lbrace 0 \right\rbrace^{d - k}\bigr).$$

Here $\left\lbrace 0 \right\rbrace^{d - k}$ denotes the singleton containing the zero vector of $\mathbb{R}^{d - k}$. Thus $\mathbb{R}^k \times \left\lbrace 0 \right\rbrace^{d - k}$ is the coordinate $k$-plane inside $\mathbb{R}^d$. Such a chart is called a slice chart or adapted chart. In these coordinates, $S$ is locally obtained by setting $d - k$ coordinates equal to zero.

The tangent space of $S$ is naturally a linear subspace of the ambient tangent space:

$$T_pS \subseteq T_pM.$$

11.2 Regular level sets#

The regular-level-set theorem gives a powerful way to construct submanifolds. Let $M$ and $N$ be smooth manifolds of dimensions $d$ and $m$, respectively, let $F : M \to N$ be smooth, and let $q \in N$. If

$$dF_p : T_pM \to T_qN$$

is surjective for every $p \in F^{-1}(q)$, then $q$ is a regular value, and $F^{-1}(q)$ is an embedded submanifold of dimension $d - m$. Moreover,

$$T_pF^{-1}(q) = \ker dF_p.$$

The level set $F^{-1}(q) = \left\lbrace p \in M : F(p) = q \right\rbrace$ is the set of points mapped to $q$. Surjectivity means that every vector in $T_qN$ is $dF_p(v)$ for at least one $v \in T_pM$. The notation $\ker dF_p$ denotes the kernel, the vectors $v \in T_pM$ for which $dF_p(v) = 0_{T_qN}$, where $0_{T_qN}$ is the zero vector in $T_qN$.

For the sphere, define $F : \mathbb{R}^{d + 1} \to \mathbb{R}$ by $F(x) = \lVert x \rVert^2$. Then $S^d = F^{-1}(1)$ and

$$dF_p(v) = 2p \cdot v.$$

For $p \in S^d$, this linear map is nonzero and hence surjective onto $\mathbb{R}$. Therefore $S^d$ is a smooth submanifold of dimension $d$, and

$$T_pS^d = \ker dF_p = \left\lbrace v \in \mathbb{R}^{d + 1} : p \cdot v = 0 \right\rbrace.$$

This recovers the tangent-space calculation from Section 7 in a more general framework. The result used here is also called the regular level set theorem or regular value theorem; see Lee (2012, Chapter 5).

11.3 Manifolds with boundary#

For $d \geq 1$, a manifold with boundary is locally modeled on relatively open subsets of the closed half-space

$$\mathbb{H}^d = \left\lbrace (x^1, \ldots, x^d) \in \mathbb{R}^d : x^d \geq 0 \right\rbrace.$$

The symbol $\mathbb{H}$ stands for half-space, and the condition $x^d \geq 0$ selects the closed side of the coordinate hyperplane $x^d = 0$. A boundary chart is a homeomorphism $\varphi : U \to \varphi(U) \subseteq \mathbb{H}^d$, where $\varphi(U)$ is open in the subspace topology of $\mathbb{H}^d$. Smoothness of transition maps is defined by requiring local smooth extensions to open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^d$. A point corresponding to $x^d \gt 0$ is an interior point; a point corresponding to $x^d = 0$ is a boundary point. This classification is independent of the chosen boundary chart. The symbol $\partial M$ denotes the set of all boundary points of $M$; it is a smooth $(d - 1)$-manifold without boundary.

For example, the closed interval $[0, 1]$ is a one-dimensional manifold with boundary

$$\partial[0, 1] = \left\lbrace 0, 1 \right\rbrace.$$

The closed ball

$$\overline{B}^d = \left\lbrace x \in \mathbb{R}^d : \lVert x \rVert \leq 1 \right\rbrace$$

is a $d$-manifold with boundary $\partial \overline{B}^d = S^{d - 1}$. The overline in $\overline{B}^d$ indicates that the boundary sphere is included; equivalently, this is the closed unit ball. It is not a manifold without boundary, because a neighborhood of a boundary point is not homeomorphic to an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$. For the precise extension-based definition and the invariance of boundary, see Lee (2012, Chapter 1).

12. What smooth structure provides—and what it does not#

A smooth manifold is already rich enough to support:

  • smooth real-valued functions and smooth maps;
  • smooth curves and their velocities;
  • tangent and cotangent bundles;
  • differentials and pullbacks;
  • vector fields, one-forms, and higher differential forms;
  • Lie brackets of vector fields;
  • ordinary differential equations and local flows.

All of these constructions are canonical consequences of the smooth structure: a smooth map automatically has a differential, a smooth function automatically has a one-form $df$, and two vector fields automatically have a Lie bracket. No metric or ambient Euclidean space is needed.

Other familiar notions require additional choices:

Desired notionAdditional structure usually required
Lengths, angles, distances, and gradientsA Riemannian metric
Spacetime intervals and causal conesA Lorentzian metric
Parallel transport and covariant differentiationA connection
Integration of top-degree formsAn orientation; alternatively, a density for orientation-free integration
Complex-analytic coordinatesA complex structure
Hamiltonian vector fields and symplectic areaA symplectic form

These extra structures are not generally unique. The same smooth manifold can carry many Riemannian metrics, connections, symplectic forms, or complex structures—and sometimes it can carry none of a requested kind. Choosing one therefore adds geometric information that cannot be recovered from the smooth atlas alone.

In particular, a bare smooth manifold has no canonical distance, no canonical notion of orthogonality, and no canonical gradient vector. It tells us what it means to differentiate, but not how to measure. This is why $df$ appeared before the gradient: $df$ belongs to smooth calculus itself, whereas $\operatorname{grad}f$ belongs to Riemannian geometry.

The hierarchy is

$$\text{set} \longrightarrow \text{topological space} \longrightarrow \text{topological manifold} \longrightarrow \text{smooth manifold} \longrightarrow \text{manifold with additional geometry}.$$

Each arrow represents additional data or additional axioms. Passing to the right preserves the earlier notions while making new questions meaningful. For example, topology lets us ask whether a sequence converges; smooth structure lets us differentiate; a metric lets us measure lengths and angles.

13. Common misconceptions#

“A manifold must be a surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$.”#

No. A manifold is defined intrinsically by its topology and compatible charts. A surface embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is an important example, not the general definition.

“A chart is one coordinate.”#

Not exactly. A chart $(U, \varphi)$ supplies an entire local coordinate system. Its component functions $x^1, \ldots, x^d$ are the individual coordinates.

“The dimension of a manifold is its ambient dimension.”#

No. Dimension counts independent local coordinates. The sphere $S^2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ has intrinsic dimension two.

“Every subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is automatically a manifold.”#

No. Singularities, crossings, corners, and changes of local dimension can violate the local Euclidean condition. Some subsets are manifolds with boundary or corners; others are not manifolds at all.

“A smooth manifold must have one global coordinate system.”#

No. Most interesting manifolds require multiple charts. Smooth geometry is designed precisely to combine local coordinate calculations into global statements.

“A tangent vector is an arrow joining two points of the manifold.”#

No. A tangent vector is based at one point and represents an infinitesimal velocity there. In general, two points of a manifold cannot be subtracted to produce a tangent vector.

“The differential $df$ and the gradient $\operatorname{grad}f$ are the same.”#

Not without a metric. The differential is a covector supplied by the smooth structure; the gradient is a vector obtained from that covector after choosing a Riemannian metric.

14. Final perspective#

A smooth manifold is not “a curved Euclidean space” in the sense of requiring a surrounding space or a metric. It is a space whose local pieces can be described by Euclidean coordinates and whose coordinate descriptions agree through smooth transition maps.

The logical architecture is now visible:

  1. topology provides neighborhoods and continuity;
  2. charts provide local Euclidean descriptions;
  3. transition maps make the coordinate descriptions compatible;
  4. the resulting smooth structure defines smooth functions and maps;
  5. curves and derivations produce tangent vectors;
  6. duality produces cotangent vectors and differentials;
  7. smooth sections produce vector fields and differential equations.

Coordinates are indispensable computational tools, but an intrinsic object or property must transform consistently when the chart changes. The true object is the manifold; coordinates are temporary languages used to describe it.

The central message can therefore be sharpened to

$$\boxed{\text{A smooth manifold is a globally defined space on which local Euclidean calculus is coordinate independent.}}$$

That local-to-global principle is the foundation on which the rest of differential geometry is built.
