Radiation transport problems involve the study of how radiant energy propagates through a background medium and the processes by which it interacts with matter.

Radiation Model#

A photon is the fundamental particle that carries radiant energy. The energy of a single photon is \(h\nu\), and its momentum is \(\frac{h\nu}{c}\), where \(c\) is the speed of light and \(h\) is Planck’s constant. At a fixed time \(t\), the motion of a photon is characterized by its position \(x\in\mathbb{R}^3\), direction of propagation \(\Omega\in\mathbb{S}^2\), and frequency \(\nu\in\mathbb{R}\). Here, \(\mathbb{R}^3\) denotes three-dimensional Euclidean space, and \(\mathbb{S}^{2}\) denotes the unit sphere embedded in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Radiant energy can be characterized by the distribution of the number of photons. Let the phase-space variable be

\[ z = (x,\Omega,\nu), \]

The distribution function of photons is

\[ f(t,x,\Omega,\nu). \]

The energy distribution of a radiation field can be described by the radiation intensity \(I\)

\[ I(t,x,\Omega,\nu) = c h \nu f(t,x,\Omega,\nu). \]

Its physical meaning is: the energy carried by radiation propagating at time \(t\), at position \(x\), along direction \(\Omega\), with frequency \(\nu\), per unit time, per unit area, per unit solid angle, and per unit frequency interval.

The transport of radiant energy can generally be broken down into two fundamental mechanisms: free transport and photon–medium interactions. On the one hand, photons move freely through the medium, causing radiant energy to propagate through physical space; on the other hand, as photons move, they interact with the surrounding medium, resulting in energy exchange between the radiation field and the medium. The primary processes involved include:

Absorption#

When photons pass through a medium, they are absorbed and disappear, causing energy to be transferred from the radiation field to the medium. The absorption process can be described by the absorption coefficient

\[ \sigma_a(t,x,\nu) \]

which gives the absorption probability per unit path length.

Scattering#

After passing through a medium, a photon changes its direction of motion and frequency and continues to travel. The scattering process preserves the total number of photons but redistributes their distribution in the direction and frequency spaces. If the frequency of the photons remains constant during scattering, it is called coherent scattering; if the scattering is isotropic in the direction, it is called isotropic scattering. The scattering process can be described by the scattering coefficient

\[ \sigma_s(t,x,\Omega\cdot\Omega',\nu\to\nu') \]

which describes, per unit path length, scattering from direction \(\Omega\) and frequency \(\nu\) into direction \(\Omega'\) and frequency \(\nu'\).

Emission#

Due to thermal motion or other microscopic mechanisms, a medium generates new photons, causing energy to be transferred from the medium to the radiation field. For isotropic media, the emission process can be described by the source term

\[ S(t,x,\nu) \]

which describes the photon emission rate at frequency \(\nu\).

Under the combined effect of the aforementioned physical mechanisms, the temporal evolution of the radiation intensity \(I\) can be described by the following radiation transport equation:

\[ \frac{1}{c}\partial_t I + \Omega\cdot\nabla_x I = \mathcal{C}_s(I) + \mathcal{C}_a(I). \]

The scattering operator is (isotropic coherent scattering)

[ \mathcal{C}_s(I)

\sigma_s(\Pi(I)-I), ]

[ \Pi(I)

\frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{\mathbb{S}^2} I, d\Omega. ]

The absorption and emission operators are

[ \mathcal{C}_a(I)

\sigma_a I + S. ]

The radiation intensity \(I\) to be solved in the radiation transport equation is a seven-dimensional function dependent on time \(t\), position \(x\), direction \(\Omega\), and frequency \(\nu\); calculating it directly is extremely computationally expensive. Therefore, we need to perform dimensionality reduction.

Multigroup Model#

In terms of frequency, there are two main methods for reducing the dimensionality of the radiation transport equation: the multigroup model and the multiband model. Both methods reduce model complexity by dividing the frequency range into several subintervals; the difference lies in the basis for this division: the multigroup model divides the range based on frequency, while the multiband model divides it based on optical properties of the medium, such as the absorption coefficient.

For the multigroup model, start with the frequency interval

\[ [0,+\infty) \]

truncate it to a finite interval

\[ [0,\nu_{\max}], \]

and further subdivide it into

\[ [\nu_{g-\frac12},\nu_{g+\frac12}], \quad g = 1,2,\dots,G, \]

where

\[ \nu_{\frac12}=0, \quad \nu_{G+\frac12}=\nu_{\max}. \]

Integrating the radiation transport equation with respect to \(\nu\) over \([\nu_{g-\frac12},\nu_{g+\frac12}]\) yields the equation corresponding to the \(g\)th frequency interval:

[ \frac{1}{c}\partial_t I_g + \Omega\cdot\nabla_x I_g

\sigma_{s,g}(\Pi(I_g)-I_g)#

\sigma_{a,g} I_g + S_g. ]

where

[ I_g

\int_{\nu_{g-\frac12}}^{\nu_{g+\frac12}} I, d\nu, ]

[ S_g

\int_{\nu_{g-\frac12}}^{\nu_{g+\frac12}} S, d\nu. ]

The scattering coefficient and the absorption coefficient are defined as

[ \sigma_{s,g}

\frac{\int_{\nu_{g-\frac12}}^{\nu_{g+\frac12}} \sigma_s(\Pi(I)-I), d\nu} {\int_{\nu_{g-\frac12}}^{\nu_{g+\frac12}} (\Pi(I)-I), d\nu}, ]

[ \sigma_{a,g}

\frac{\int_{\nu_{g-\frac12}}^{\nu_{g+\frac12}} \sigma_a I, d\nu} {\int_{\nu_{g-\frac12}}^{\nu_{g+\frac12}} I, d\nu}. ]

Specifically, when \(G=1\), the corresponding model is called a gray model; when only a specific frequency subinterval is considered, it is called a one-group model. The remainder of this paper will focus primarily on radiation transport models in the one-group case.

Macroscopic Diffusion Limit#

To analyze the relative importance of different physical mechanisms in the evolution of the system, we will now nondimensionalize the radiation transport equation. Let the mean free path of photons, the characteristic spatial scale, and the characteristic temporal scale be \(\bar{\ell}\), \(\bar{x}\), and \(\bar{t}\), respectively, and introduce the following dimensionless variables and parameters:

\[ \begin{aligned} x &= x'\bar{x}, \\ t &= t'\bar{t}, \\ \sigma_s &= \frac{\sigma_s'}{\bar{\ell}_s}, \\ \sigma_a &= \frac{\sigma_a'}{\bar{\ell}_a}, \\ S &= \frac{S'}{\bar{\ell}_q}. \end{aligned} \]

The primed quantities represent the corresponding dimensionless variables, while \(\bar{\ell}_s\), \(\bar{\ell}_a\), and \(\bar{\ell}_q\) represent the mean free paths for scattering, absorption, and emission events, respectively.

Introduce the dimensionless parameter \(\varepsilon\), which represents the ratio of the mean free path of a photon to the characteristic spatial scale, defined as

[ \varepsilon

\frac{\bar{\ell}}{\bar{x}}. ]

To study the long-term evolutionary behavior of the system, we stretch the time dimension, that is,

[ \varepsilon \bar{t}

\frac{\bar{x}}{c}. ]

This indicates that the observation timescale is much longer than the time required for a photon to traverse a macroscopic spatial scale.

In addition, we are particularly interested in the transport behavior of radiant energy in optically thick regions. In such regions, the mean free path for scattering is much shorter than the mean free paths for absorption and emission:

[ \frac{\bar{\ell}}{\bar{\ell}_s}

1, ]

[ \frac{\bar{\ell}}{\bar{\ell}_a}

\frac{\bar{\ell}}{\bar{\ell}_q}#

\varepsilon^2. ]

Based on the above assumptions, we substitute the dimensionless transformation into the radiation transport equation and drop the primes to simplify the notation, yielding the following multiscale form of the radiation transport equation:

[ \varepsilon^2 \partial_t I + \varepsilon \Omega\cdot\nabla_x I

\sigma_s(\Pi(I)-I)#

\varepsilon^2 \sigma_a I + \varepsilon^2 S. ]

To analyze the asymptotic behavior of this model at \(\varepsilon \to 0\), we use a formal Hilbert expansion, writing

[ I

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \varepsilon^k I^{(k)}, ]

Substituting into the above equation yields equations of various orders.

Order \(O(1)\)#

[ I^{(0)}

\Pi(I^{(0)}). ]

Order \(O(\varepsilon)\)#

[ \Omega\cdot\nabla_x I^{(0)}

\sigma_s \left( \Pi(I^{(1)})

I^{(1)} \right). ]

Order \(O(\varepsilon^2)\)#

[ \partial_t I^{(0)} + \Omega\cdot\nabla_x I^{(1)}

\sigma_s \left( \Pi(I^{(2)})

I^{(2)} \right)

\sigma_a I^{(0)} + S. ]

Next, apply the angular variable averaging operator to the above equation \(O(\varepsilon^2)\)

[ \langle \cdot \rangle

\frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{\mathbb{S}^2} (\cdot), d\Omega, ]

Combining this with the equations of the first two orders, we can conclude that \(I^{(0)}\) satisfies the following diffusion equation:

[ \partial_t I^{(0)}

\nabla_x\cdot \left( \frac{1}{3\sigma_s} \nabla_x I^{(0)} \right)

\sigma_a I^{(0)} + S. ]

In summary, under diffusion-scale transformation, when \(\varepsilon \to 0\), the multiscale radiative transport model formally converges to a macroscopic diffusion model.
